The framing decision that runs through everything

Early on we fixed the comparison discipline: "best baseline" means the best trained method — the constrained-optimization duals (Fioretto-LDF, ALM, Hounie) — never post-hoc clipping. Clipping is reported as its own category: it can match cc-F1 by editing predictions after the fact, but it never satisfies the cap natively.

This matters because comparing TraLO to clip at short warm-up is meaningless — clip is undertrained there, so any gap measures "clip didn't train," not a real advantage. Holding this line kept the headline honest and, as it turned out, the paper's own framing already matched it — so Track B strengthens the manuscript with no contradiction to resolve.

Field guide · start here

The problem: meeting a hard prediction quota

Every result in this project turns on one deployment situation. You have a trained classifier and a known, fixed set of inputs it must label — say, today's batch of skin-lesion images. A downstream constraint says: you may flag at most K of them as the sensitive class (melanoma, a disease, a disaster). Not because the model wants to, but because something real is limited — a specialist can only review so many cases, a budget only covers so many follow-ups, a safety protocol only tolerates so many alarms.

That "at most K over this specific set" is called a transductive count constraint. Transductive is a term from Vapnik: instead of learning a general rule and hoping it behaves on unseen data, you reason directly about the specific cases in front of you. The cap isn't "predict melanoma 8% of the time in general" — it's "over these 500 images, output at most 40 melanoma labels." The count is over a concrete prediction set, so it can be measured and enforced exactly.

There are two honest ways to meet such a cap, and the whole thesis is about which is better:

1 · Edit the predictions after training (post-hoc clipping). Train a normal classifier, then, if it flagged too many, un-flag the least-confident ones until you're under K. Simple, always works — but it never learned anything about the cap; it just trims the output.

2 · Train the network so its own predictions respect the cap (TraLO and the "dual" baselines). Add a term to the loss that punishes going over K, so the network learns a decision boundary that already satisfies the quota. The claim we test: when the cap actually bites and the model still has room to learn, training to satisfy beats editing after the fact — and even when it only ties on the score, it has the advantage of satisfying the cap natively, with no post-hoc surgery.

Track B is eight experiments stress-testing exactly when that claim holds and when it collapses to a tie. To read them fluently, you need the vocabulary below.

Field guide · the methods

The cast: every method, and where its name comes from

Two families compete. The constraint-training methods (ours + three "duals") bake the cap into the loss. The baselines of convenience (clipping + three imbalanced-learning tricks) attack the same symptom differently. Names in this field are almost always the author of the paper the method comes from.

The constraint-training family — they learn to satisfy the cap

TraLOOurs

Name = "transductive loss" — the method is the extra loss term that enforces the transductive count cap.

penalty = E/(E+K)  +  ρ·(E/K)² / (1 + (E/K)²)   where E = max(0, softcount − K)

What it does: trains with cross-entropy plus a bounded count penalty. "Bounded" matters: both terms saturate (they can't blow up to infinity), so a badly-scaled penalty can't wreck training. It's driven by controllers described in Training — a rising penalty weight, a reset trick, an on/off hinge. The whole thesis is: this specific, portable recipe beats the classical alternatives when the cap binds.

Fioretto-LDFDual baseline

Named after Ferdinando Fioretto's Lagrangian Dual Framework for constrained deep learning.

dual update: λ ← [ λ + η·(S − K) ]₊   (S = current count, K = cap)

What it does: the textbook constrained-optimization approach. Keep a multiplier λ (the "price" of violating the cap) and raise it by a step η whenever you're over, lower it when under — a linear feedback loop. Clean and standard, but the linear update can overshoot and wind up (λ keeps climbing), which is the weakness the next method fixes.

ALMDual baseline · the tough one

Not a person — Augmented Lagrangian Method, a classical optimization technique (Hestenes/Powell, 1969).

λ ← max(0, λ + η·(S−K)) + μ·(S−K)⁺,   with μ growing over time

What it does: the standard literature fix for Fioretto's windup. It adds a quadratic term with a penalty weight μ that grows, which stabilizes the multiplier. This is the strongest, fairest dual baseline — if anything should close the gap to TraLO, it's this. B3 exists specifically to check whether it does. (It doesn't.)

Hounie-RCLDual baseline

Named after Hounie et al.'s Resilient Constrained Learning.

What it does: lets the constraint relax when meeting it is too costly — it trades a little violation for better accuracy, adaptively. Elegant in general, but at tight caps it collapses (its cc-F1 drops to ~0.285 while everyone else is ~0.45): when the quota is severe, "resiliently relaxing" means largely giving up on the constraint. A useful failure — it shows tight caps are a genuinely hard regime.

The convenience baselines — they attack the symptom without constraint-training

Post-hoc clipping"clip" · Shifman-LP in the paper

"LP" = the small linear program that picks which predictions to flip; renamed Shifman-LP in the manuscript.

What it does: train a completely normal classifier, then after the fact flip the fewest borderline predictions needed to get under the cap. It always satisfies K by construction — but only by editing outputs, never natively. This is the crucial comparator: it can match the constrained-class score by trimming, so "we beat clip on the score" is a claim we hold to a very high bar (and mostly it's a tie). What clip can never do is satisfy the cap natively — its native-satisfaction rate is 0.0, TraLO's is 1.0.

Focal lossImbalanced-learning · Lin 2017

From Lin et al. 2017 (RetinaNet). "Focal" = it focuses learning on the hard examples.

ℓ = −α · (1 − p_y)^γ · log p_y   (α = 0.25, γ = 2)

What it does: down-weights easy, already-correct examples (the (1−p_y)^γ factor shrinks as confidence p_y grows), so training concentrates on the rare, hard class. It's the one convenience baseline that genuinely competes on overall quality — on the imbalanced Derm dataset it ties or beats TraLO's macro-F1 (B1).

Class-balancedImbalanced-learning · Cui 2019

From Cui et al. 2019 — reweight classes by their "effective number" of samples.

weight w_c = (1 − β) / (1 − β^{n_c})   (β = 0.9999, n_c = class count)

What it does: up-weights rare classes, but with diminishing returns (adding the 1000th example of a class helps less than the 10th). A refined version of "just weight by inverse frequency." In our results it mostly ties — a reasonable trick that doesn't move the needle here.

Logit-adjustmentImbalanced-learning · Menon 2020

From Menon et al. 2020 — shift the output logits by the (log) class priors.

z_c ← z_c + τ · log π_c   (π_c = class prior, τ = 1)

What it does: nudges each class's score by how common it is, so rare classes need less evidence to win. A principled, training-free adjustment — but in our regime it's the weakest of the three (often slightly negative), because our bottleneck is the count cap, not the prior.

Field guide · the metrics

The metrics: what the numbers actually measure

Three numbers carry every verdict. The trick to reading them: accuracy is useless when the class you care about is rare, so we never report it — we report F1, split two ways, plus whether the cap was met without cheating.

cc-F1

Constrained-class F1 — the F1 score computed on only the capped class (melanoma, the disease). F1 is the harmonic mean of precision (of the cases I flagged, how many were right) and recall (of the real cases, how many I caught). We use F1 not accuracy because the class is ~5–8% of the data: a model that never flags anything scores 92%+ accuracy and is useless. cc-F1 is the headline learning-quality number.

macro-F1

The unweighted average of F1 across all classes. Where cc-F1 zooms in on the one capped class, macro-F1 asks "did enforcing the cap wreck the other classes?" It's the overall-quality check — and it's where focal loss is a real competitor.

native satisfaction

Did the model's raw argmax predictions already meet the cap, with no post-hoc editing? Reported as a rate: 1.0 = always satisfied natively (all constraint-trained methods), 0.0 = never (clipping only satisfies K after its editing step). This is TraLO's durable, backbone-independent edge — even in every tie, it's the method that meets the quota by itself.

cap notation
L30_G30

The caps, as a percent of the class's true prevalence. G = the global cap over the whole set; L = a local per-group cap (e.g. per demographic/anatomical group). L30 = "allow only 30% as many positives as truly exist" — a very tight quota; L70 is loose. Tighter caps force harder choices, which is exactly where constraint-training earns its keep.

Why the scores have a ceiling — and why that matters

If the cap only lets you output 30% as many positives as really exist (L30), then even a perfect model can recall at most 30% of the true cases. With perfect precision that caps F1 at 2c/(1+c) — for c = 0.30, that's ≈ 0.46. So when you see cc-F1 values around 0.41 at L30, the methods aren't failing — they're pressed against a mathematical ceiling.

This is why so many comparisons are ties: at tight caps the ceiling crushes everyone together, and at loose caps there's no pressure to differentiate. The wins live in the mid-cap band (L30–L40) where the cap bites but the ceiling hasn't flattened everything — a clean inverted-U.

Field guide · the training machine

The training machine: three phases and their guardrails

TraLO isn't one loss — it's a training procedure with controllers that keep the constraint from destabilizing learning. Understanding these is what lets you explain why ablations (B4) and the mechanism make sense.

The three phases

1 · Warm-up

Train with cross-entropy only for W epochs — get a competent classifier before mentioning the cap. W is the single most important lever in Track B: a short warm-up leaves the model still learning ("headroom"); a long warm-up (W=50) lets it saturate first. Headroom is what the whole mechanism hinges on.

2 · Constraint phase

Now add the count penalty: CE + λ·penalty. The network keeps learning to classify while being pushed under the cap. This is where all the controllers below operate.

3 · Post-hoc adjust

A final safety net: if the hard count still exceeds K, flip the fewest borderline predictions — global cap first, then per-group local caps, then re-verify. For a well-trained model this barely fires; for clipping it is the whole method.

The guardrails (and the problem each one solves)

λ ratchet

The penalty weight λ climbs each epoch until the cap is satisfied, then freezes. Turns up the pressure gradually instead of shocking training with a huge penalty on day one.

ρ schedule

The quadratic term's weight ρ rises linearly until first satisfaction, then holds. Same idea, for the second penalty term — ease in, then lock.

optimizer reset

The moment the cap is first met, wipe Adam's momentum. Without this, built-up momentum keeps shoving predictions past the cap, hurting recall. Ablations show this is the single most load-bearing component in the winning regime.

hinge / λ-toggle

Turn the penalty off when satisfied, back on when violated (with oscillation detection). Keeps the constraint gradient from fighting cross-entropy once the cap is already met — preserves gradient balance.

CE-saturation skip

If train accuracy hits 0.995, stop the constraint phase — there's no headroom left to re-rank anything, so continuing just wastes compute (and, per the mechanism, can't change the ranking anyway).

convergence stop

Cap satisfied for 5 epochs straight → stop early. This is also a result: how fast each method converges is a reported comparison (TraLO converges first-or-tied; Hounie is slowest).

The one subtlety worth knowing: soft counts vs hard counts

The loss can't use the actual count of predictions — argmax isn't differentiable, you can't take a gradient through it. So training optimizes a soft count (the sum of predicted probabilities for the class), which is differentiable. But satisfaction is judged on the hard count (the real argmax labels).

That gap between "what the loss pushes on" and "what actually gets measured" is the project's known limitation — and it's exactly what the phase-3 post-hoc step exists to close.

Field guide · the statistics

How we decide a result is real

A single lucky run proves nothing. The rules below are what separate a genuine effect from noise — and following them is why several early "wins" got honestly demoted to ties.

the cell

The atomic unit of evidence is one (dataset, backbone, cap, method) combination, averaged over 4 random seeds. Every number in the tables is a cell. Cells are never pooled across backbones or datasets — averaging a ViT with a MobileNet hides where an effect actually lives.

paired testing

TraLO vs a baseline is always compared at matched seed — same seed means same initialization and same data order, so the only difference is the method. This isolates the effect far better than comparing pooled averages with independent noise.

bootstrap CI

To put error bars on a gap, we resample the cells 20,000 times and read off the 95% interval. If that interval excludes 0, the effect is real; if it includes 0, it's a tie — no matter how nice the mean looks. This is what confirmed the dual win (B6) and what demoted the clip "win."

BH-FDR

Benjamini-Hochberg false-discovery-rate correction (q = 0.05). When you run many comparisons, some pass by chance; FDR controls for that. The dual comparison survives it; the clip comparison doesn't (B7) — so we only claim significance for the duals.

win-bar τ

A cell only counts as a "win" if TraLO leads by more than a margin τ. B5 re-checks every verdict at τ = 0.003, 0.005, 0.010 — a result that flips depending on the threshold isn't robust. The dual win holds at all three; the clip win never appears.

Why this discipline is the point

These rules are why the report reads "one clear win, two disciplined claims" instead of "TraLO wins everywhere." Early analyses that pooled backbones or skipped the paired test showed TraLO beating clip — and those claims did not survive the paired, multi-backbone, bootstrap-corrected re-analysis. Reporting the tie is not a weakness; it's the evidence that the remaining win (vs the duals) is trustworthy.

The spine

One mechanism explains every win and every tie

The count penalty reaches the weights only through a scalar soft-count, so its gradient is a scalar times a fixed direction — its scale (λ, ρ) is absorbed by Adam plus gradient clipping. What it can do then depends entirely on whether cross-entropy is still active.

CE active — tight cap + headroom

The penalty re-ranks

With cross-entropy still learning, the penalty moves borderline examples across the decision boundary — a real cc-F1 gain over the trained duals.

→ wins: B3 (ALM), B8 (MNV2)

CE saturated — loose cap / long warm-up / easy data

The penalty only shifts logits

Once CE is saturated, the penalty applies a uniform logit shift — same top-K, so the ranking, and the score, don't move. A tie.

→ ties: B2 native, B1 cc-F1, B4 tie-region, B5–7 vs clip

Post-hoc clipping sits on a separate axis entirely: it can match cc-F1 by editing predictions, but it can never satisfy the cap natively.

Why the penalty's scale doesn't matter — the Adam scale-invariance argument

Here's the part that surprised us and became the theory. The penalty touches the weights only through that one scalar soft-count S — so its gradient is a single number times one fixed direction in weight space. Because that penalty term takes its own step through the Adam optimizer, and Adam divides every gradient by its own running magnitude, multiplying the penalty by any constant cancels out. We verified it directly: scaling the penalty 3× versus 53× changed the actual weight update by 0.000e+00.

This is why so many "tuning" experiments came back null — λ, ρ magnitude, the penalty shape: all absorbed. What can't be absorbed is the direction, and whether that direction can still move the decision boundary depends entirely on whether cross-entropy has left any headroom. Same fixed direction + headroom = re-rank (a win); + no headroom = uniform shift (a tie). One idea, every outcome.

Bottom line

Track B produced one clear, well-supported win — TraLO beats both constrained-optimization duals, including the strong ALM, on OctMNIST tight caps, bootstrap-significant across four backbones — and confirmed the headroom mechanism at native resolution and in a tie region.

It also disciplined two claims: clipping is a tie plus native satisfaction, not an accuracy win; and focal ties TraLO's macro-F1 on DermMNIST. Framed as a precise characterization of when transductive count-constraint training helps, this is a more defensible contribution than a universal-win claim — and it is exactly the framing the paper already carries.
