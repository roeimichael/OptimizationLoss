\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A surprising number of classification systems are deployed under a constraint
not on which items get a label but on \emph{how many} do. A screening program
reads a fixed pool of scans and may escalate only so many of them to
specialist review; an audit team can act on only so many flagged filings in a
quarter. In each case the operational requirement is a
\emph{hard upper bound on the count} of predictions in a constrained class over
the evaluation set. The items to be labeled are known in advance (a fixed
batch to be scored, not a stream), so the constraint is naturally
\emph{transductive}: it is a property of the whole test-set prediction vector,
not of any single example.

The standard way to honor such a cap is \emph{post-hoc clipping}: train a normal
model, rank the test items by their score for the constrained class, keep the
top-$K$, and reassign the rest. This is trivial and always meets the count, so
it is the realistic deployment baseline. But it meets the count by editing
predictions, often dozens of them, each a forced error on an item the model was
confident about, so accuracy degrades. The other end of the spectrum is
\emph{constrained training}: encode the cap as a constraint and solve the
Lagrangian with dual ascent
\citep{fioretto2020lagrangian,hounie2023resilient,chamon2020probably}. In
principle this is exactly the right formulation, but for a hard count the dual
multiplier of a linear penalty climbs without bound: on a one-epoch-warmup
probe (App.~\ref{app:mech}) it climbs steeply while a bounded multiplier
barely moves, for the same final count. The implementations remain competitive
and we do not over-read the escalation; it is a symptom, not the damage. What
it marks is a formulation that on some cells never reaches feasibility at all
(Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}).

\paragraph{This paper.} We characterize a third answer that lands on the count
by itself, \del{without prediction surgery}{\color{blue}with an order of
magnitude less prediction surgery}, and \emph{we identify which of its
ingredients actually do the work}. The package we study --- for exposition we
refer to it as TraLO (\emph{Transductive Loss Optimization}) --- combines a
saturating transductive penalty (a rational-saturation term plus a bounded
quadratic in the soft excess over the cap) with a multiplier that only moves
upward while the count is violated and freezes the first time it is met, an
Adam-state reset at first satisfaction, and an undershoot hinge that restores
constrained-class recall once the cap is met. The package as a whole meets the
{\color{blue}global} count natively on essentially every run
{\color{blue}\del{--- and needs an order of magnitude fewer per-group edits than any
clipper ---}} and beats post-hoc clipping on macro-F1
in nearly every regime. But the internal decomposition matters more than the
brand: our ablation and graft show that the constrained-class win rests on
\emph{two} of these ingredients --- the optimizer reset and the undershoot
hinge --- and that they transfer to the dual baselines, while the saturating
penalty shape after which the package is named proves neutral.

\paragraph{Who should care.}
\emph{Practitioners} building screening, audit, or triage pipelines under
hard batch caps on MedMNIST-scale tasks: this paper reports when
constraint-time training gains $1.6$--$5.3$ percentage points of overall
quality over satisfying the cap after training{\color{blue} with an
imbalance-unaware clipper}, at a compute cost of a few
extra minutes per run. \del{Whether the same margin holds at native imaging
resolution is not addressed here.}
{\color{blue}The same margin holds at native imaging resolution on the one
dataset we tested there (App.~\ref{app:native}), and against a focal-trained
clipper it holds on two of the three datasets (Sec.~\ref{sec:deploy}).}
\del{\emph{Researchers on constrained deep learning}: this paper isolates two
portable ingredients (an Adam-state reset at first feasibility and an
undershoot hinge) that transfer across constraint-trained methods and,
under matched training budgets, close most of the tight-cap gap between
them; the shape of the enclosing penalty proves neutral by both ablation
and graft.}

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{An empirical characterization of when constrained training
  wins.} We formalize the operational ``$\le K$ predictions of class $c$''
  requirement as a transductive count constraint (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}) and
  map, over a $1944$-run grid, the regimes where constraint-trained methods
  beat clipping on macro-F1 (nearly all of the grid;
  Sec.~\ref{sec:deploy}) and where they beat one another on
  constrained-class F1 (essentially: OctMNIST tight caps;
  Secs.~\ref{sec:oct}--\ref{sec:regime}).
  \item \textbf{Two portable components that carry the constrained-class advantage.}
  An ablation isolates an Adam-state reset at first feasibility and an
  undershoot hinge (App.~\ref{app:ablation}) as load-bearing;
  a cross-method graft (Sec.~\ref{sec:carries}) shows they transfer to
  Fioretto-LDF \citep{fioretto2020lagrangian} and Hounie-RCL
  \citep{hounie2023resilient} and close most of the tight-cap gap. Neither
  component is derived; both are motivated empirically and identified by
  ablation.{\color{blue} Both are measured in a constrained-class F1 that is
  \emph{not} equalized for realized count (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}), so on the
  present evidence ``carries the advantage'' and ``makes the model spend its
  count budget'' are not separated.}
  \item \textbf{The saturating penalty shape is quality-neutral.} The
  saturating penalty after which the package we study is named is neutral
  to constrained-class quality: swapping it for a linear penalty at the
  same ratcheted multiplier moves cc-F1 by $-0.001$ across $24$ runs,
  with $6$ runs landing on identical predictions
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:carries}; App.~\ref{app:shape}). Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}
  earns its place by keeping the frozen multiplier small and readable,
  not by earning the quality gain.
  \item \textbf{A public regime map and per-run corpus.} We release the
  full $1944$-run grid (per-cell, per-seed) and the code, so any of the
  headline claims --- including the negative and tie regions --- is
  independently verifiable at the granularity we report them.
\end{itemize}

\label{sec:related}

\paragraph{Output-level and fairness constraints.}
Closer to our output-level setting, prediction constraints have been enforced
during training by primal--dual updates \citep{nandwani2019primal} and by
linear label-count constraints for weakly supervised segmentation
\citep{pathak2015constrained}, or guaranteed per prediction by
input-dependent parametrizations \citep{brosowsky2021sample} or an
output-layer satisfiability solver \citep{goyal2024deepsade}.
\citet{marquez2017imposing} find hard enforcement feasible but no better than
soft penalties, motivating our bounded penalty. Constrained optimization has
protected under-represented classes \citep{sangalli2021constrained}, and
group-wise constraints underpin fair classification
\citep{bilal2015fairness,agarwal2018reductions,kearns2018preventing,donini2018empirical},
which our local caps resemble structurally, though a local cap is a
\emph{ceiling}, not a fairness floor (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}).

\paragraph{Rate-constrained learning.}
The closest classical relatives constrain prediction \emph{rates}:
coverage as a dataset constraint \citep{goh2016satisfying}, non-decomposable
rate objectives through surrogate bounds \citep{eban2017scalable},
non-differentiable rate constraints through proxy-Lagrangian games
\citep{cotter2019optimization}, and generalized rate metrics through
multi-player schemes \citep{narasimhan2019optimizing}. These bound
\emph{expected} rates over a distribution, inductively; ours is a hard count
on one specific test set, verified on the argmax predictions and paired with
per-group local caps.

\paragraph{Post-hoc correction and resource allocation.}
Resource-constrained classification allocates a fixed budget over a trained
classifier's outputs at decision time, classically as a small assignment
problem, most recently under exactly our local-plus-global structure
\citep{shifman2025classification}. Related lines fold the budget into learning via adaptive cost-sensitive
weights \citep{shifman2023adaptive} or predict-and-optimize ranking
under stochastic capacity \citep{vanderschueren2024new}.
Learning-with-rejection \citep{cortes2016learning} instead prices
abstention per instance. All steer expected costs rather than a hard
count. Decision-time allocation always
meets the budget by editing predictions; our post-hoc baselines are a greedy
threshold heuristic and a linear-programming clipper (LP-LG), following
the local-and-global allocation formulation of
\citet{shifman2025classification}.

\paragraph{Cost-sensitive and imbalanced training.}
Reweighting or reshaping the loss changes which examples are emphasized:
cost-sensitive thresholds \citep{elkan2001foundations}, focal loss
\citep{lin2017focal}, class-balanced weights \citep{cui2019class},
label-distribution-aware margins \citep{cao2019learning}, prior-corrected
logits \citep{menon2020long}. None of these guarantees the prediction count, which the
constraint-trained methods studied here target directly. \del{Whether a
well-tuned imbalanced-training recipe followed by clipping would close
the macro-F1 gap over vanilla clipping is a question our current
experiments do not settle \mbox{(Sec.~\ref{sec:limits})}.}
{\color{blue}Whether a well-tuned imbalanced-training recipe followed by
clipping closes the macro-F1 gap is answered in Sec.~\ref{sec:deploy}: focal
loss does close it on DermMNIST, but not on TissueMNIST or OctMNIST, so the
advantage is dataset-dependent rather than uniform.}

\paragraph{Transductive and set-level constraints.}
Transductive inference reasons about a fixed unlabeled test set
\citep{vapnik1998statistical,joachims1999transductive}, where soft set-level
targets have long existed: expectation regularization pushes predicted class
proportions toward a prior \citep{mann2007simple}, and posterior
regularization constrains posterior expectations \citep{ganchev2009posterior}.
Our constraint is transductive in this sense but \emph{hard}: an explicit
argmax count budget, with per-group caps, enforced during training.

\paragraph{Few methods target both scopes at once.}
The families surveyed above address either a single global constraint
(dual-ascent methods, rate-constrained learning, expectation
regularization) or per-group constraints framed as fairness floors (fair
classification), but rarely both a global count cap and per-group local
caps that must hold at the same time on argmax predictions over a
specific test set. Under exactly this local-plus-global structure, the
closest recent work is the post-hoc allocator of
\citet{shifman2025classification}. On the constraint-trained side we
know of no baseline that handles both scopes jointly, so our benchmark
implementations of Fioretto-LDF and Hounie-RCL required adaptation to
enforce a global count together with per-group ceilings on argmax
counts (Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}).

\section{Problem and Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let the (known) test set contain $N$ items and let $p_\theta(y=c\mid x_i)$ be
the model's probability for the constrained class $c$ on item $i$. A
\emph{global} count constraint requires the number of items predicted $c$ to
satisfy $\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[\hat y_i = c] \le K$. We parameterize the
cap as a percentage $L$ of the constrained class's true test-set count, so
$L$ measures how much of the class's actual demand the quota admits ($L30$
admits at most $30\%$ of the true count). \emph{Local} caps also bound the
count within each group $g$: $\sum_{i\in g}\mathbf{1}[\hat y_i=c]\le K_g$
(the same percentage of the group's own class count). Because the indicator
is not differentiable, training uses the \emph{soft count}
$S=\sum_i p_\theta(y=c\mid x_i)$, while satisfaction is verified with the
\emph{hard count} from $\arg\max$.

\paragraph{Overview of the package.}
\del{The package we study --- for exposition we refer to it as \emph{TraLO}
(Transductive Loss Optimization) --- combines}{\color{blue}The package, \emph{TraLO}
(Sec.~\ref{sec:intro}), combines} four ingredients: a saturating
per-constraint penalty (below); a multiplier that ratchets while the count is
violated and freezes the moment it is met; an Adam-state reset at first
satisfaction; and an undershoot hinge that engages once the count falls below
the cap. Not all four ingredients carry equal weight\del{. Sec.~\ref{sec:carries}
and App.~\ref{app:ablation}--\ref{app:shape} show that the constrained-class
advantage over the strongest dual-ascent baselines rests specifically on the
reset and the hinge, that both transfer as portable additions to the dual
baselines, and that the penalty shape after which the package is named is
neutral by both ablation and graft. We describe the four ingredients below
before returning to the empirical story.}{\color{blue}, as Sec.~\ref{sec:carries}
and App.~\ref{app:ablation}--\ref{app:shape} establish; the four are described
below.}

\paragraph{The constraint penalty.}
For a soft excess $E = \max(0,\, S - K)$ over a cap $K$, the per-constraint
penalty is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\ell(E;K,\rho)\;=\;\underbrace{\frac{E}{E+K}}_{\text{rational saturation}}
\;+\;\rho\,\underbrace{\frac{(E/K)^2}{1+(E/K)^2}}_{\text{bounded quadratic}} ,
\end{equation}
where both terms saturate as $E\!\to\!\infty$ (the first to $1$, the second
to $\rho$). What actually drives training is the penalty's \emph{gradient}
on the count; differentiating Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} through $E=\max(0,S-K)$
gives, for $S>K$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:grad}
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial S}
= \frac{K}{(E+K)^2} + \frac{2\rho}{K}\,\frac{E/K}{\bigl(1+(E/K)^2\bigr)^2}.
\end{equation}
Both the penalty and this gradient are bounded (App.~\ref{app:mech}). The
total objective combines cross-entropy with, per cap, the overshoot penalty
and an undershoot hinge $h$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total}
L_{\text{total}} = L_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{G}}\,(\ell_{\mathrm{G}} + h_{\mathrm{G}}) + \lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\,(\ell_{\mathrm{L}} + h_{\mathrm{L}}) ,
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{\mathrm{G}}=\ell(E_{\mathrm{G}};K_{\mathrm{G}},\rho)$ is the global-cap penalty and
$h_{\mathrm{G}}=\beta\max(0,\,K_{\mathrm{G}}-S_{\mathrm{G}})/K_{\mathrm{G}}$ its hinge (weight $\beta{=}0.5$, discussed below):
identically zero while $S\ge K$, hence inert throughout the violated phase,
it engages only once the soft count falls below the cap, in practice after
first satisfaction. $\ell_{\mathrm{L}}$ and $h_{\mathrm{L}}$ sum the same two terms over the
group caps; every cap (the global one and each group cap) carries its own
multiplier, so $\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\,(\ell_{\mathrm{L}}+h_{\mathrm{L}})$ abbreviates
$\sum_g \lambda_g\bigl(\ell(E_g;K_g,\rho)+\beta\max(0,K_g-S_g)/K_g\bigr)$, the sum running over groups $g$.

{\color{blue}A note on subscripts, since three of them are easy to conflate.
\del{\emph{This revision renames them throughout this section}: what earlier read
$\lambda_g,\ell_g,h_g$ and $\lambda_l,\ell_l,h_l$ is now written with upright
$\mathrm{G}$ and $\mathrm{L}$, because the old lowercase $g$ collided with the
group index in the local terms. The symbols changed; no definition did.}
Upright $\mathrm{G}$ and $\mathrm{L}$ mark the global and the pooled local terms;
lowercase $g$ indexes a \emph{group}, so $K_g$ is group $g$'s cap and
$\lambda_g$ its multiplier. Where a statement holds for an arbitrary cap --- the
global one or any group's --- we index it by $c$ and call it a constraint, as in
$S_c$, $K_c$ and $\lambda_c$ below and throughout App.~\ref{app:mech}; the
constrained class itself is always named in words rather than by symbol.}

A cap's penalty depends on the parameters only through its scalar soft count
$S_c$, so its gradient always points along the same fixed direction
$\nabla_\theta S_c$: the multiplier, $\rho$, and the penalty's shape scale
that push but cannot redirect it, and under gradient clipping even the scale
cancels \citep{pascanu2013difficulty}. This is a partial argument only:
$\lambda$ multiplies only the constraint term and thus shifts the
cross-entropy-versus-constraint weighting in the combined update, so the
multiplier scale is not provably inert; and the shape reshapes $\ell'(S_c)$
at each $E$ rather than rescaling it, so it falls outside the scalar-rescaling
argument entirely. A matched probe with $\lambda$ differing ${\sim}280\times$
trains almost identically (App.~\ref{app:mech}), so the near-neutrality is
empirical rather than theoretical. So the saturating penalty of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} sets the \emph{scale} of the constraint push, not the
update it produces: its shape is neutral by ablation and graft
(Sec.~\ref{sec:carries}), and the quality comes instead from Adam's reset
and the undershoot hinge, which change the direction.

\paragraph{Multiplier ratchet and freeze.}
Rather than dual ascent, $\lambda$ is \emph{ratcheted}: it increases by a
fixed step each epoch \emph{only while the hard count violates the cap}, and
is \emph{frozen} the moment the count is first satisfied. The bounded
penalty means the search ends at a small frozen multiplier rather than an
escalating one; multipliers start at $0.05$. The quadratic weight $\rho$
ramps linearly (from $5$ toward $100$) until first satisfaction and then
also freezes. Because $\lambda$ and $\rho$ both stop moving at first
satisfaction, the training procedure is really two-phase: while the count
is violated it \emph{searches} for a sufficient multiplier by ratcheting
$\lambda$ (the bounded gradient of Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad} keeps that search
ending at a small $\lambda$), and once the count is met it \emph{settles}.

\paragraph{Stability, and why a verification step is retained.}
Freezing $\lambda$ and $\rho$ at first satisfaction turns the moving-target
dual problem into a single fixed objective
$F(\theta)=L_{\text{CE}}+\lambda_{\mathrm{G}}(\ell_{\mathrm{G}}{+}h_{\mathrm{G}})+\lambda_{\mathrm{L}}(\ell_{\mathrm{L}}{+}h_{\mathrm{L}})$:
the tail of training descends one objective instead of chasing a multiplier
that is still moving. Because the penalty has a nonzero slope ($1/K$) at the
cap, a finite frozen multiplier suffices to pin the soft count there rather
than merely discourage violations, which is why the package reaches the cap
natively once cross-entropy saturates (App.~\ref{app:mech}). Feasibility is
defined by the \emph{hard} count while the penalty acts on the \emph{soft}
count, so a verification step (App.~\ref{app:deploy}) closes that small
residual gap.

\paragraph{Optimizer reset at satisfaction.}
At the first epoch the count is met, we reset the Adam optimizer state. This
removes the accumulated descent momentum that would otherwise keep pushing
the soft count far below the cap (over-satisfying at the cost of
constrained-class recall). The reset lets the frozen-multiplier objective
settle near the boundary instead of overshooting inward. It is motivated
empirically, not derived\del{, and other reset variants (partial reset of only
first or only second moments, or decay-based) are not covered by our
experiments;}{\color{blue}, and the reset variants our experiments do not cover
are set out in Sec.~\ref{sec:limits};} the ablation (App.~\ref{app:ablation}) isolates the effect of
the full reset used here. The reset is applied only at the
constraint-satisfaction phase transition, and is distinct from continuous
learning-rate restart schedules \citep{loshchilov2017sgdr}, which are
orthogonal to the multiplier ratchet.

\paragraph{Undershoot hinge.}
A satisfied cap can still leave the model under-predicting the constrained
class, which costs recall. The hinge term $h$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:total}
counters this: each cap $c$ contributes
$\lambda_c\,\beta\,\max(0,\,K_c-S_c)/K_c$, zero whenever the soft count sits
at or above its cap, so it can never re-violate
{\color{blue}the \emph{soft} constraint}; once the soft count drops
below the cap it gently pushes it back up toward (but not over) the cap,
restoring constrained-class recall.
{\color{blue}The guarantee is soft-count-only, and worth stating as such:
feasibility is verified on the hard count (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}, Problem), and
the two can disagree, so in principle the hinge can engage while a cap is still
hard-violated and push the soft count further toward it. We did not observe this
delaying feasibility --- TraLO is never the slowest to reach the count in any of
the $18$ census cells (Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}) --- but that is evidence, not a
proof.} App.~\ref{app:ablation} shows the hinge
and the reset together carry the effect.

\paragraph{Training schedule.}
Training has a warmup phase (cross-entropy only, to a stable initialization
that is cached and reused) followed by the constraint phase under
Eq.~\eqref{eq:total}. A post-hoc verification step is available to enforce a
hard count if a run has not landed natively; for the reset+hinge package it
is essentially never needed \del{(App.~\mbox{\ref{app:deploy}})}{\color{blue}\emph{for the
global cap} (App.~\ref{app:deploy}). The per-group caps are a different matter: under
the joint criterion (global met \emph{and} zero edits) the package lands natively on
$0.21$ of the grid and $0.60$ at binding caps, so local limits still require some
editing}. Algorithm~\ref{alg:tralo}
summarizes the loop.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{The training loop of the package studied here.}
\label{alg:tralo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\State warm up $\theta$ on $L_{\text{CE}}$ alone; cache the warmup model
\For{each constraint epoch}
  \State update $\theta$ on $L_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{G}}\,\ell_{\mathrm{G}} + \lambda_{\mathrm{L}}\,\ell_{\mathrm{L}}$ \del{(plus the hinge once satisfied)}{\color{blue}(plus the hinge, carried from the first constraint epoch whenever $\lambda>0$; it is identically zero while $S_c\ge K_c$)}
  \State recount hard predictions on the test inputs
  \If{a cap is violated}
    \State ratchet that cap's $\lambda$ up one step; advance $\rho$
  \ElsIf{first time all caps are met}
    \State freeze $\lambda$ and $\rho$; reset the Adam state
  \EndIf
  \State stop after $5$ consecutive satisfied epochs
\EndFor
\State post-hoc verification (flip borderline predictions) if needed
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

{\color{blue}
\paragraph{The verification step, specified.}
Every method's reported predictions come from the same routine, so we state it
rather than leaving it as ``flip borderline predictions.'' Starting from the
argmax labelling it runs three greedy phases in order. \emph{(1)~Global
eviction}: for each constrained class over its cap, the lowest-probability
predictions of that class are reassigned, each to its own highest-probability
class that is not itself at a limit. \emph{(2)~Global fill}: for each
constrained class under its cap, the highest-probability non-members are
promoted --- but only from samples whose current class is unconstrained or
itself over-limit, and only in groups that are not already at their per-group
cap. That second gate is why a run can end below $K$ even though the phase
exists. \emph{(3)~Per-group enforcement}: the same eviction-then-fill within
each group. A small-scope linear program is run only if the greedy phases leave
a violation, and the global caps are re-checked afterwards. \textbf{Flips} counts
every reassignment made by these phases. The \textbf{Heuristic} baseline is the
same allocation applied directly to the warm-up model's probabilities with no
training phase: constrained classes are filled first, tightest cap first, each
taking its top-scoring unassigned samples, and every remaining sample takes its
best still-feasible class. Finally, \textbf{seeds $1$--$4$ index four distinct
warm-up trainings}: the seed enters the warm-up cache key, so each seed is a
separate trained model that all methods then share. Every paired comparison in
this paper is therefore between methods that started from the same weights, and
$n{=}4$ means four independent trainings, not four restarts from one.
}

(App.~\ref{app:graft}). A third experiment (App.~\ref{app:shape})
substitutes a linear penalty for Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} to isolate the
contribution of the penalty shape itself.

\paragraph{Ablation isolates the reset and the hinge.}
On the six OctMNIST tight-cap cells where TraLO leads, we disable one
component at a time and measure the paired change in constrained-class F1
($n{=}24$ per component; {\color{blue}App.~\ref{app:ablation}, }Table~\ref{tab:ablation_complete}). Two
components are load-bearing. Removing the Adam-state reset at satisfaction
costs $+0.079$ cc-F1, the single largest effect, helping on all $24$
paired runs and hence in all six cells (cell-level sign test $p\!=\!0.031$).
\del{Removing the undershoot hinge (the TraLO-bounded control, already in the
main grid) costs \mbox{$+0.036$} cc-F1 (positive on all \mbox{$24$} paired runs;
cell-level sign test \mbox{$p\!=\!0.031$}; \mbox{$0.019$--$0.070$} across cells).}
{\color{blue}Removing the undershoot hinge alone, with the reset retained, costs
$+0.032$ cc-F1: it helps in $22$ of the $24$ paired runs and in all six cells
(cell-mean gaps $+0.009$ to $+0.056$; cell-level sign test $p\!=\!0.031$). Removing
both together --- the TraLO-bounded control already in the main grid --- costs
$+0.036$, which is \emph{less} than removing the reset alone: the two are not
additive, because they repair overlapping failures rather than independent ones.}
The other components are cc-F1-neutral on these already-satisfied cells: the
$\rho$ schedule and the lambda freeze move it by under $0.001$ (n.s.).

\paragraph{Graft transfers the win to the dual baselines.}

{\color{blue}
The obvious objection to that finding is that we withheld the ingredients from

On these cells TraLO and ALM-plus-the-two-components are the same method to

and what a paper arguing for its own package would least like to find.}

\paragraph{Penalty shape is neutral.}
A third arm swaps Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}'s saturating penalty for a linear
one at the same ratcheted multiplier, holding the ratchet, the freeze,
the reset, and the hinge fixed --- so the only change is the shape after
which the package is named. The arm is genuinely different (its logged
constraint loss runs about $3\times$ smaller throughout); its
predictions are not. cc-F1 moves by $-0.001$ (the bounded shape wins $9$
of $24$ runs and loses $9$, with $6$ exact ties; sign-test $p{=}1.0$)
and median time-to-feasibility by two epochs. In $6$ of the $24$ runs the
two arms land on \emph{identical} cc-F1: changing the penalty shape
changed no prediction at all, consistent with the fixed-direction
argument of Sec.~\ref{sec:method}. We report this as a negative result
about our own formulation: \del{Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} earns its place by keeping
the frozen multiplier small and readable, not by earning the
constrained-class gains, which belong to the reset and the hinge.}%
{\color{blue}Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} sets the scale, and the constrained-class
gains belong to the reset and the hinge.}

\paragraph{Anti-windup as a mechanistically-limited control.}
As a further control, Fioretto-LDF with dual restarts
\citep{gallego2022controlled} deploys predictions \emph{identical} to
plain Fioretto-LDF in every cell, because the deployed checkpoint is
selected on the constraint-excess axis before first feasibility and the
restart machinery only acts after feasibility. The control therefore
cannot in principle change the deployed model. What it does show is that
the linear-penalty comparison in the main grid is not a strawman created
by omission of a standard post-feasibility fix; it does not rule out
that a stronger anti-windup scheme acting before feasibility could close
the gap.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

We foreground the limitations because several of them are load-bearing for
how the reader should generalize.

\paragraph{Dataset scope.}
All three datasets are drawn from MedMNIST v2 and are $28{\times}28$
images upsampled to $224{\times}224$ for the ImageNet-pretrained
backbones. Every headline claim in this paper is scoped to that setting.

long-tailed benchmarks (ImageNet-LT), or to text/graph settings. The
regime map itself may re-derive at other scales --- the mechanistic story
(dual ascent overshoots downward, hinge restores recall) is not
resolution-specific --- but this is a hypothesis, not an evidenced claim.

{\color{blue}We take one step past the upsampled setting in App.~\ref{app:native}:

\paragraph{The advantage over trained baselines is regime-specific.}
Only two regimes clear the win bar: OctMNIST tight symmetric caps (the

\paragraph{Motivation--result mismatch on OctMNIST.}
The screening motivation in Sec.~\ref{sec:intro} imagines rare, high-stakes
classes. OctMNIST's drusen class is neither: the test split is
class-balanced by construction ($250$ items per class), and drusen is a
relatively benign finding beside CNV or DME. The OctMNIST cells earn their

not because the story matches deployment, and the result is not evidence about
rare-class clinical screening.

\paragraph{Confound between constraint-time training and test-batch
adaptation.} The macro-F1 gain over post-hoc clipping is claimed for the
constraint-trained family in aggregate. But constraint-time training on
the test batch (transductive fitting) is a second thing our methods do
that vanilla clipping does not, and the current experiments do not
separate the two: some of the +$1.6$--$5.3$\,pp macro-F1 gain plausibly
comes from test-batch adaptation rather than from avoided editing per

separate them; we did not run it.

\paragraph{Transductive-only scope.}
The formulation is transductive: it enforces a hard count on a known
evaluation batch, scored in one shot. Online/streaming settings where
items arrive one at a time and the count budget must be spent over time
are outside the scope of this work. A natural extension would replace
the ratcheted global multiplier with a running budget-consumption
controller and the transductive count with an expected residual on the
remaining stream, but we do not evaluate this here.

\paragraph{Naming caveat.}
The package is named for its saturating penalty shape
(Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}), which the ablation and graft show to be neutral to
constrained-class quality\del{. Readers looking to reuse the
findings should carry the reset and the hinge, not the specific penalty
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}: it is a scale-setter, not a quality-setter.}%
{\color{blue}: a scale-setter, not a quality-setter. What transfers is the
reset and the hinge.}

\section{Discussion and Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Count caps are a real, common deployment constraint whose two standard
answers each cost something: post-hoc clipping pays an accuracy tax for
editing predictions, and dual-ascent training chases the cap with an
escalating multiplier and, in its linear-penalty form, can stall short of
it. A third answer --- constrained training with a saturating penalty, a
multiplier frozen at satisfaction, an optimizer reset, and an undershoot
hinge --- matches the strong constraint-trained baselines across the grid
and beats them on the constrained class where the cap binds hard and the
class stays discriminable (OctMNIST tight caps; preliminarily,
DermMNIST's global-tighter asymmetric caps),{\color{blue} including against an
augmented-Lagrangian dual that is itself stronger than plain dual ascent, and on a
fourth backbone,} and improves macro-F1 over
\del{post-hoc clipping}{\color{blue} imbalance-unaware post-hoc clipping}
while landing on the count natively.{\color{blue} That last margin is the most
qualified of the three: against a clipper trained with focal loss it holds on two of the
three datasets rather than everywhere, and it reproduces at native
$224{\times}224$ resolution on the one dataset we ran to full depth. What holds
without qualification, at every cap and on every backbone, is that the
{\color{blue}\emph{global}} count is met
during training rather than by editing predictions afterward{\color{blue}; the
per-group caps still require some editing, an order of magnitude less than any
clipper's (App.~\ref{app:deploy})}.}

Two findings of this paper are worth stating separately from the
package. First, the constrained-class advantage over the strongest
dual-ascent baselines rests on \emph{two} specific ingredients --- an
Adam-state reset at first feasibility and an undershoot hinge --- that
transfer to the dual baselines and close most of the gap, while the
saturating penalty after which the package is named proves neutral by
both ablation and graft.{\color{blue} Each is load-bearing on its own, not only as
a pair: removing either alone costs constrained-class F1 in all six tight-cap
cells, though the hinge's single-component row is paired across campaigns and so
is provisional (Sec.~\ref{sec:carries}). The transfer is not confined to the
weaker hosts --- grafted onto the augmented-Lagrangian dual, the strongest
baseline we run, the two components close the gap entirely: TraLO's remaining
edge is $+0.0002$ cc-F1 on $11$ of $24$ seeds.} Second, the regime map shows that hard-count
constrained training pays back over clipping essentially everywhere on
our grid, while beating the trained baselines only in a narrow,
identifiable regime; the map is what a practitioner needs, not the
brand of any one package. We release the full corpus so both findings
--- and every tie and negative result behind them --- are independently
verifiable at the granularity we report them.

\subsubsection*{Broader Impact Statement}
This paper studies methods that enforce hard prediction quotas in
classification systems. Such quotas often arise in screening, audit, or
triage pipelines where downstream capacity (specialist time, investigator
attention) is limited. There are two potential harms worth flagging.
First, a hard cap on flagging a class is a \emph{ceiling} on how many
items are escalated, not a floor on how well the population is served;
misapplied as a fairness intervention, per-group caps can lock in
under-diagnosis of groups where the cap is set from a biased prevalence
estimate. Section~\ref{sec:related} and App.~\ref{app:groups} distinguish
the ceiling reading from a fairness floor, but we cannot control how
downstream users set $K_g$. Second, we evaluate on MedMNIST-derived
tasks (28$\times$28 upsampled); the paper does not license clinical
deployment on the datasets we use, and readers should not read the
OctMNIST result as evidence about rare-class clinical screening
(Sec.~\ref{sec:limits}). Beyond these two, the constrained-training
recipe has no capabilities beyond ordinary supervised classification and
no new dual-use concerns that we can identify.

\section{Mechanism: Bounded vs.\ Unbounded Dual Behavior}
\label{app:mech}

\del{This appendix supports the convergence and mechanism story of
Sec.~\ref{sec:conv} in two ways: first, it establishes analytically that
the saturating penalty and its gradient are bounded and that a finite
frozen multiplier suffices to pin the soft count at the cap; second, it
reports a one-epoch-warmup probe that isolates the constraint-descent
dynamics from the cross-entropy saturation of the paper grid, exposing
where dual ascent applies force it cannot use.}

{\color{blue}
\paragraph{Bounded penalty and gradient.}
Write $u=E/K\ge 0$, with $E=\max(0,S-K)$. Equation~\eqref{eq:loss} is a sum of two
increasing terms,
\[
\ell \;=\;
\underbrace{\frac{E}{E+K}}_{=\,u/(1+u)\,:\;0\to 1}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\frac{\rho\,u^{2}}{1+u^{2}}}_{0\,\to\,\rho}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
0\le\ell<1+\rho .
\]
Differentiating through $E$ gives Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad} for $S>K$, and its two summands are
bounded separately:
\[
\frac{K}{(E+K)^{2}}=\frac{1}{K}(1+u)^{-2}\;\le\;\frac{1}{K},
\qquad
\frac{2\rho}{K}\cdot\frac{u}{(1+u^{2})^{2}}\;\le\;\frac{9\rho}{8\sqrt3\,K}\;<\;\frac{\rho}{K},
\]
the first decreasing in $u$, the second maximised at $u=1/\sqrt3$; both tend to $0$ as
$u\to\infty$. Hence the gradient is bounded and vanishes far from the cap,
\[
0\;\le\;\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial S}\;\le\;\frac{1+\rho}{K},
\qquad
\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial S}\to 0 \ \text{ as } E\to\infty .
\]
Scaling $\ell$ and $\partial\ell/\partial S$ by a frozen multiplier $\lambda$ gives the
bounds $\lambda(1+\rho)$ and $\lambda(1+\rho)/K$, neither depending on $E$.
}

{\color{blue}
\paragraph{Rank-one gradient direction.}
The penalty reaches the weights only through the scalar soft count:
$E_c=\max(0,S_c-K_c)$ depends on $\theta$ solely via $S_c$, so
$\lambda_c\ell(E_c)$ is the composition
$\theta\mapsto S_c(\theta)\mapsto\lambda_c\ell$. The chain rule then gives a
\emph{non-negative scalar times a fixed direction},
\[
\nabla_\theta\bigl[\lambda_c\,\ell\bigr]
\;=\;
\underbrace{\lambda_c\,\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial S_c}}_{=:\;a_c\;\ge\;0}
\;\nabla_\theta S_c ,
\qquad\text{using}\qquad
\frac{\partial E_c}{\partial S_c}=\mathbf 1[S_c>K_c] .
\]
Summing over the caps,
\[
\sum_c \nabla_\theta\bigl[\lambda_c\,\ell\bigr]
\;\in\;
\operatorname{span}\{\nabla_\theta S_c\}_c ,
\]
a subspace of dimension at most the number of caps.
}

{\color{blue}
\paragraph{Gradient clipping absorbs the scalar.}
Write the full update direction as
\[
g \;=\; \nabla_\theta L_{\text{CE}} \;+\; \sum_{c'} a_{c'}\,\nabla_\theta S_{c'} .
\]
In the limit where one cap's term dominates and the rest vanish, $g\to a_c\nabla_\theta S_c$
with $a_c>0$. Norm-clipping, whenever it is active ($\lVert g\rVert>\tau$), returns
\[
\tau\,\frac{g}{\lVert g\rVert}
\;\longrightarrow\;
\tau\,\frac{\nabla_\theta S_c}{\lVert\nabla_\theta S_c\rVert},
\]
in which $a_c$ has cancelled --- and with it $\lambda_c$, $\rho$, and the shape of $\ell$.
}

{\color{blue}
\paragraph{A finite multiplier suffices.}
Write $g(S)=\ell\bigl(\max(0,S{-}K);K,\rho\bigr)$. It is flat below the cap but has a
nonzero one-sided slope above it:
\[
g\equiv 0 \ \ (S<K)
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad
g'(K^-)=0,
\qquad
g'(K^+)=\lim_{S\to K^+}\left[\frac{K}{(E+K)^{2}}+\frac{2\rho}{K}\frac{u}{(1+u^{2})^{2}}\right]
=\frac{1}{K},
\]
with $E=S-K$ and $u=E/K$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad}) --- note the limit is independent of $\rho$.
Parameterise a path along which the soft count rises through $K$, and let
$\kappa=\lvert dL_{\text{CE}}/dS\rvert_{S=K}$ be the cross-entropy pull at the cap (the
constrained class is under-served at any cap $L<100\%$, so $dL_{\text{CE}}/dS\le0$ there).
The one-sided derivatives of $F=L_{\text{CE}}+\lambda g$ at $K$ are then
\[
F'(K^-)=-\kappa\le 0,
\qquad
F'(K^+)=-\kappa+\frac{\lambda}{K}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
F'(K^+)\ge 0
\ \ \text{ whenever }\ \
\lambda\ge \kappa K .
\]
So $F$ does not decrease past the cap: $S{=}K$ is a local minimum along the path, and a
\emph{finite} multiplier attains it. The nonzero boundary slope $1/K$ is what makes the
penalty exact, playing the role of the $\ell_1$ penalty's unit slope
\citep{nocedal2006numerical}.

Three caveats, so the statement is not read for more than it says. It assumes
$\kappa$ is finite, which holds wherever the cross-entropy is differentiable at the
cap but is an assumption, not a consequence. It assumes
$dL_{\text{CE}}/dS\le0$ at $K$: the constrained class is under-served at any cap
$L<100\%$ in aggregate, but App.~\ref{app:groups} notes that on TissueMNIST and
DermMNIST the warmup model already predicts the constrained class at close to
its capped rate, which is exactly the regime where the sign is weakest --- and
those are the two datasets on which we report a tie rather than a win. And
``local minimum'' is along the parameterised path only; it is not a local
minimum in $\theta$, and nothing here claims one.
}

\medskip
Figure~\ref{fig:loss_shape} plots these two bounds against the violation
size. Why does a hard count cap stress the dual-ascent baselines? On the
paper grid the interaction is hidden: after the $50$-epoch warmup the
classifier is already saturated, and a saturation gate shared by every
method (train accuracy $\ge 0.995$ on two consecutive checks) stops
cross-entropy updates within the first two constraint epochs, so the
constraint phase is pure count optimization for all methods alike. We
therefore probe with a one-epoch warmup, where the classifier keeps
learning throughout the constraint phase and the two objectives visibly
compete (Figure~\ref{fig:mechanism}).

The probe shows three things. First, while the classifier is learning,
the count cannot be forced down: each epoch's cross-entropy updates
re-inflate the constrained class, and the excess oscillates around its
starting level for ${\sim}60$ epochs under both methods
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}c). Dual ascent responds exactly as designed:
the violation persists, so the multiplier grows every epoch, from $1$ to
$53$ (seeds $2$--$3$: $58$, $60$). In control terms this is integrator
windup: the controller keeps integrating an error it has no ability to
act on. Second, the escalation buys nothing. Every method takes a
single gradient-clipped constraint step per epoch (max-norm $1.0$,
shared across methods), so extra multiplier cannot buy extra descent:
the two cross-entropy traces are numerically indistinguishable
(Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}b), and the count comes down no faster at
$\lambda{=}53$ than at TraLO's ratcheted $\lambda\le 0.19$. \del{The
asymmetry is in what the clip rail is doing. At $\lambda{=}53$ an}%
{\color{blue}The asymmetry is at the clip rail: at $\lambda{=}53$ an}
unbounded-penalty gradient would dominate the update by orders of
magnitude were it not clipped, whereas TraLO's bounded penalty at
$\lambda\le 0.19$ demands orders of magnitude less force in the first
place. Third, the count falls only once the classifier saturates and the
gate stops CE updates (dotted lines in Fig.~\ref{fig:mechanism}c), and
then both methods reach the cap within a few epochs. The count outcome is
identical; what differs is the pressure applied to the constrained class
to get there.

The paper grid shows the same behavior in compressed form: the dual
multipliers escalate while TraLO's freezes ($\le 0.09$ there),
Fioretto's native satisfaction is ${\sim}0.87$ ($0.84$ on OctMNIST and
TissueMNIST, $0.93$ on DermMNIST), so its unbounded multiplier stalls
short of the cap on ${\sim}13\%$ of runs that never reach feasibility
within the budget (Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}); runs that end violated restore
their best-satisfied checkpoint and remain competitive.

Nor is the escalation a step-size artifact. A dedicated fairness sweep
(Block~C of the released corpus; $96$ runs on the hard-regime cells)
varies Fioretto's dual step over $0.001$--$0.01$ and Hounie's $\eta$
over $0.001$--$0.1$: Fioretto's constrained-class F1 moves by less than
$0.001$ across the $10\times$ step range, larger Hounie steps only hurt
($0.43\!\to\!0.35$), and no setting closes the gap to TraLO on the
OctMNIST tight cells (the best variant still trails by $0.019$ cc-F1).
{\color{blue} Two qualifications, because this sweep is the paper's answer to
the charge that the baselines are under-tuned. It is MobileNetV3-only, so it
does not speak for the ViT-B/16, where TraLO's margin is largest. And Hounie is
in fact markedly $\eta$-sensitive: at $\eta{=}0.001$ it reaches $0.386$ and
$0.480$ on the two tight caps against roughly $0.29$ and $0.40$ at
$\eta{\in}\{0.05,0.1\}$. What makes the conclusion hold anyway is that even at
its best setting Hounie still trails Fioretto-LDF ($0.391$ and $0.487$), and we

Sec.~\ref{sec:method} showing up in a baseline we did not design.}

We state this mechanism \emph{qualitatively only}. An audit shows that
the magnitude of the escalation does not predict where TraLO wins (the
multiplier is in fact largest in some cells where TraLO loses). So the
claim is ``dual ascent needs an unbounded multiplier plus a clip rail
to hold a hard count, while TraLO's bounded, frozen multiplier holds it
alone,'' not any dose--response of the form ``more escalation
$\Rightarrow$ bigger win.''

\subsection{The penalty shape is neutral}
\label{app:shape}

\del{This subsection is the source of the paper's negative penalty-shape

constraint penalty depends on the parameters only through the scalar

accumulated state, and the hinge, which reverses the direction to
$-\nabla_\theta S_c$ while the count undershoots: these are the
components the ablation ranks first and the graft transfers.

\subsection{TraLO's own hyperparameters are not doing the work}
\label{app:tralo-sweep}

\del{This subsection answers a natural robustness question: are TraLO's own
tunable knobs (the hinge weight $\beta$ and the ratchet step

The dual baselines' step size is swept an order of magnitude to confirm
their tuning is not what wins or loses the comparison

which Adam's normalization and the clip rail absorb.

clears the best trained baseline in all $30$ arm-by-cell comparisons,
with margins from $+0.014$ to \del{$+0.088$}{\color{blue}$+0.083$}: the tight-cap advantage
survives every setting of the two knobs, not only the values we fixed
a priori.

\subsection{The convergence ordering is not an initialization artifact}
\label{app:init}

\del{This subsection rules out that TraLO's faster feasibility over the dual
baselines (Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}) is an artifact of its non-zero multiplier

baselines start at $\lambda{=}0$ and integrate upward, so one could
read TraLO's faster feasibility (Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}) as a head start

tight cells by rerunning each dual baseline from TraLO's own
$\lambda{=}0.05$ start, changing nothing else ($24$ runs each). The
nonzero start leaves Fioretto-LDF's median time-to-feasibility

count faster than either a zero- or a nonzero-start dual ascent. This
control is specific to the OctMNIST tight cells; the cross-dataset
census of Sec.~\ref{sec:conv} uses each method as published.

\paragraph{What the three tasks contain.}
\del{All three come from MedMNIST v2 \mbox{\citep{yang2023medmnist}} with their official
splits, as $28{\times}28$ images upsampled to $224{\times}224$ for the
ImageNet-pretrained backbones.}

\textbf{TissueMNIST} is human kidney-cortex microscopy
\citep{woloshuk2021situ}, subsampled to $9{,}600$ training and $2{,}400$ test
images over eight cell types (CDI, CDP, CT, DCT, GE, INT, PTC, PTS). The
constrained class is GE, glomerular endothelial cells, at $171/2400 = 7.1\%$ of
the test set. Images are grayscale, replicated to three channels.

\textbf{DermMNIST} is HAM10000 dermatoscopy \citep{tschandl2018ham10000},
$8{,}012$ training and $2{,}003$ test images over seven lesion types: melanocytic
nevi (NV, $66.9\%$), melanoma (MEL, $223/2003 = 11.1\%$), benign keratosis (BKL, $11.0\%$),
basal-cell carcinoma (BCC, $5.1\%$), actinic keratoses (AKIEC, $3.2\%$),
vascular lesions (VASC, $1.4\%$) and dermatofibroma (DF, $1.1\%$). The
constrained class is melanoma. This is the only dataset with real group
metadata: anatomical site, mapped to torso ($\approx52\%$), extremity
($\approx37\%$) and head/neck ($\approx11\%$), which is what the per-group caps
use.

\textbf{OctMNIST} is retinal optical-coherence tomography from OCT-2017
\citep{kermany2018identifying}, four classes (CNV, DME, drusen, normal), with a
test split balanced by construction at $250$ per class. The
constrained class is drusen.

\paragraph{Why the three behave differently under a cap.}
Two properties of the label distributions explain most of the regime map, and
neither is visible from the class lists alone.

First, \emph{class balance differs sharply}: DermMNIST is dominated by a single
class, NV at two-thirds of the data, with the constrained class an order of
magnitude smaller, while OctMNIST's test split is balanced by construction. The
per-dataset figures are in the paragraph below.

Second, and this is the property that makes OctMNIST the hard-binding case,
\emph{OctMNIST's training and test distributions disagree}: drusen is roughly
$8\%$ of the training data but $25\%$ of the balanced test split. A model warmed
up on cross-entropy therefore \emph{under}-predicts drusen on the test set
before any constraint is applied, so a tight cap binds against a count the model
was already reluctant to produce, and the constrained class has real recall to
lose. On TissueMNIST and DermMNIST the warmup model already predicts the
constrained class at close to its capped rate, so the same cap is nearly
inactive. The regime map of Sec.~\ref{sec:regime} is largely this distinction
made quantitative: the methods separate where the cap binds against the model's
own tendency, and tie where it does not.

Local caps are only over real groups on DermMNIST. There we pool
HAM10000's \texttt{localization} field into three anatomical groups:
torso (back, trunk, abdomen, chest, genital, unknown), extremity
(upper/lower extremity, foot, hand, acral), and head/neck (face, neck,
scalp, ear), giving $1030/738/235$ test images carrying $98/85/40$
melanomas. The groups are deliberately unequal in both size and
prevalence, which is what makes the per-group caps bind differently.

TissueMNIST and OctMNIST carry no group metadata, so their local caps
use a fixed synthetic binary split drawn once from a seeded generator
and stored with the data. These groups are arbitrary by construction.
They exercise the mechanism, several simultaneous caps that must hold
at once alongside the global cap, and nothing else: no fairness
reading of the TissueMNIST or OctMNIST local caps is available, and we
do not offer one. Even on DermMNIST, where the groups are real, a
local cap is a per-group \emph{ceiling} on flagging melanoma rather
than a floor on detecting it, so satisfying it is not evidence of
equitable treatment. A deployment that set these caps from estimated
prevalence would inherit whatever under-diagnosis those estimates
already encode. We report the mechanism and leave the allocation
question, which is a question about who sets $K_g$ and on what
evidence, outside our claims.
