\begin{abstract}
We study \emph{transductive count constraints}: hard caps on how many test-set
items a classifier may assign to a constrained class (a triage queue that admits
only so many ``urgent'' cases), optionally with per-group local caps. Post-hoc clipping
meets the cap but erodes accuracy by editing predictions, and dual-ascent training escalates
an unbounded multiplier that can climb steeply and still stall short of feasibility. We
propose \textbf{TraLO}, a bounded
transductive loss whose penalty saturates and whose multiplier ratchets while the count
is violated and freezes once it is met, with an optimizer reset and an undershoot hinge
that restores constrained-class recall. Across three MedMNIST datasets and three backbones
(MobileNetV3, RegNetY-400MF, ViT-B/16), TraLO matches the strong constraint-trained
baselines across most of the grid and pulls ahead on constrained-class F1 wherever the
count binds hard: on OctMNIST tight caps the lead is backbone-general and \emph{grows with
backbone capacity}, reaching $+0.081$ on the ViT, and it recurs, preliminarily, on the
global-tighter asymmetric caps of DermMNIST. Against post-hoc clipping, the
constraint-trained family including TraLO improves macro-F1 on every dataset and backbone
by landing on the cap natively, the one exception being the dual baselines tying the
clipper on OctMNIST with the ViT. We report the full regime map, wins and ties alike, with a
component ablation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A surprising number of classification systems are deployed under a constraint not
on which items get a label but on how many do. A clinical triage tool
may escalate only a fixed fraction of scans to a specialist, and an inspection line
can act on no more flagged items than it has inspectors. In each case the operational requirement is a
\emph{hard upper bound on the count} of predictions in a constrained class over the
evaluation set. The items to be labeled are known in advance, so the
constraint is naturally \emph{transductive}: it is a property of the whole test-set
prediction vector, not of any single example.

The pervasive way to honor such a cap is \emph{post-hoc clipping}: train a normal
model, rank the test items by their score for the constrained class, keep the top-$K$,
and reassign the rest. This is trivial to implement and always meets the count, which
is why it is the realistic deployment baseline. But it meets the count by
editing predictions, often dozens of them, and every edit is a forced error
on an item the model was previously confident about, so accuracy degrades. The other
end of the spectrum is \emph{constrained training}: encode the cap as a constraint and
solve the Lagrangian with dual ascent
\citep{fioretto2020lagrangian,hounie2023resilient,chamon2020probably}. In principle this
is exactly the right formulation, but for a hard count the dual multiplier of a
linear penalty climbs without bound. While the classifier is still learning, its gradient keeps
re-inflating the constrained class, so the violation persists and the multiplier climbs
every epoch. A one-epoch-warmup probe shows it climbing steeply while our bounded
multiplier barely moves, for the same final count (Supp.~Sec.~A). The implementations
remain competitive, but the constrained class is the part of the model most stressed by
the escalation, and on some cells the dual never reaches feasibility at all
(Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}).

\label{fig:datasets}

We propose \textbf{TraLO} (Transductive Loss Optimization), a constrained-training
method whose multiplier stays bounded by construction. Its transductive penalty is
bounded (a rational-saturation term plus a bounded quadratic in the soft excess
over the cap), the multiplier only ever moves upward while the count is violated
and freezes the first time it is met, the Adam state is reset at first satisfaction
to kill post-satisfaction descent momentum, and an undershoot hinge restores
constrained-class recall once the cap is met. The result is a classifier that lands on
the count by itself, with no prediction surgery.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{A deployable formulation and method.} We cast the operational
  ``$\le K$ predictions of class $c$'' requirement as a transductive count constraint
  and give a bounded penalty with a freeze-at-satisfaction multiplier,
  optimizer reset, and undershoot hinge (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{A characterized quality win over the trained baselines.}
  Versus the much stronger constraint-trained baselines, TraLO matches
  constrained-class F1 across most of the grid and beats them wherever the count
  binds hard: on all three backbones, at OctMNIST tight caps, where the lead
  grows with backbone capacity and reaches $+0.081$ on the ViT transformer, and
  preliminarily, on two of the three backbones, at the global-tighter
  asymmetric caps of DermMNIST. We publish the full regime map including the large region
  where it is a statistical tie (Secs.~\ref{sec:oct}--\ref{sec:regime}).
  \item \textbf{A quantified accuracy tax for post-hoc clipping, and a family that
  avoids it.} Across all three datasets the constraint-trained family, TraLO
  included, improves macro-F1 over the field-default clipping baseline on every backbone
  including the ViT transformer, save a tie at OctMNIST$\times$ViT (Sec.~\ref{sec:deploy}). TraLO delivers
  the gain with native satisfaction on every run in our grid and the fewest prediction edits at
  binding caps (Supp.~Sec.~C).
  \item \textbf{Mechanism and ablation.} We separate two roles: the bounded penalty makes
  the count reachable with a small, frozen multiplier, while the constrained-class quality
  gain is carried by the optimizer reset and undershoot hinge, isolated in a complete
  ablation (Supp.~Sec.~B).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Lagrangian and dual constrained learning.}
Encoding requirements as constraints and solving with primal--dual methods is the
classical recipe \citep{bertsekas2014constrained}, brought to deep networks by
Lagrangian-duality training \citep{fioretto2020lagrangian} and given statistical
footing by PAC constrained-learning theory
\citep{chamon2020probably,chamon2023constrained}. Resilient Constrained Learning
\citep{hounie2023resilient} adapts the constraint level to make hard requirements
attainable, and PID-style dual updates stabilize the multiplier in safe RL
\citep{stooke2020responsive}, and \citet{gallego2022controlled} make the empirical
case that constraints outperform fixed penalties on sparsity targets. Our critique of
runaway escalation is therefore specific to the unbounded linear-penalty formulation:
augmented-Lagrangian and resilient updates keep the multiplier better behaved, yet the
resilient method we benchmark still winds up slowly on a hard count (Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}).
Closer to
our output-level setting, constraints on a network's predictions have been enforced
during training by primal--dual updates \citep{nandwani2019primal} and by linear
constraints on predicted label counts for weakly supervised segmentation
\citep{pathak2015constrained}, or guaranteed per prediction by construction through
input-dependent parametrizations \citep{brosowsky2021sample} or an output-layer
satisfiability solver \citep{goyal2024deepsade}; \citet{marquez2017imposing} find hard
enforcement feasible but no better than soft penalties, which motivates our bounded
penalty. Constrained optimization has also been used
to protect critical, under-represented classes \citep{sangalli2021constrained}, and
group-wise constraints underpin fair classification
\citep{zafar2017fairness,agarwal2018reductions}, the trained analogues of our local caps.
We use the two strongest dual-ascent methods, Fioretto-LDF
\citep{fioretto2020lagrangian} and Hounie-RCL \citep{hounie2023resilient}, as our
constraint-trained baselines.
Our point of departure is that a hard count cap is the setting where
the dual multiplier escalates without bound; TraLO instead optimizes a bounded
penalty and freezes the multiplier at satisfaction.

\paragraph{Rate-constrained learning.}
The closest classical relatives constrain prediction \emph{rates}:
coverage (the fraction of items predicted positive) as a dataset constraint
\citep{goh2016satisfying}, non-decomposable rate objectives through surrogate bounds
\citep{eban2017scalable}, non-differentiable rate constraints through proxy-Lagrangian
games \citep{cotter2019optimization}, and generalized rate metrics through multi-player
schemes \citep{narasimhan2019optimizing}. These bound \emph{expected} rates over a
distribution, inductively. Our requirement is a hard count on one specific test
set: transductive, verified on the argmax predictions themselves, and paired with
per-group local caps.

\paragraph{Post-hoc correction and resource allocation.}
Resource-constrained classification allocates a fixed budget over a trained
classifier's outputs at decision time, classically as a small assignment
problem, most recently under exactly our local-plus-global constraint structure
\citep{shifman2025classification}. Other work in this line folds the budget into
learning via adaptive cost-sensitive weights \citep{shifman2023adaptive} or
predict-and-optimize ranking under stochastic capacity
\citep{vanderschueren2024perspective}, steering expected costs rather than
enforcing a hard count; learning-with-rejection \citep{cortes2016learning} prices
abstention per instance, likewise without a count guarantee. In fair classification,
post-processing likewise edits a trained model's decisions with group-specific
thresholds \citep{hardt2016equality}. Decision-time
allocation always meets the budget, but it is a deploy-time clipper: it edits the
model's predictions to do so. We use a greedy threshold heuristic and a
linear-programming clipper (Shifman-LP, after the two-phase allocator of
\citealt{shifman2025classification}) as our post-hoc baselines, and show TraLO removes
the accuracy tax they pay.

\paragraph{Cost-sensitive and imbalanced training.}
Reweighting or reshaping the loss changes which examples are
emphasized: cost-sensitive thresholds \citep{elkan2001foundations}, focal loss
\citep{lin2017focal}, class-balanced weights \citep{cui2019class},
label-distribution-aware margins \citep{cao2019learning}, prior-corrected logits
\citep{menon2021logit}. None of it guarantees the prediction count; the realized
number of constrained-class predictions remains a free outcome. TraLO targets the
count directly.

\paragraph{Transductive and set-level constraints.}
Transductive inference reasons about a fixed unlabeled test set
\citep{vapnik1998statistical,joachims1999transductive}, and soft set-level targets
have long existed there: expectation regularization pushes predicted class
proportions on unlabeled data toward a prior \citep{mann2007expectation}, and
posterior regularization constrains posterior expectations
\citep{ganchev2010posterior}. Our constraint is transductive
in exactly this sense, a function of the entire test-set prediction
vector, but it is \emph{hard}: an explicit argmax count budget on a single class, with
per-group caps, enforced during training of a deep classifier.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let the (known) test set contain $N$ items and let $p_\theta(y=c\mid x_i)$ be the
model's probability for the constrained class $c$ on item $i$. A \emph{global} count
constraint requires the number of items predicted $c$ to satisfy
$\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[\hat y_i = c] \le K$. We parameterize the cap as a percentage
$L$ of the constrained class's true test-set count, so $L$ measures how much of the
class's actual demand the quota admits ($L30$: the model may assign the label to at
most $30\%$ as many items as truly carry it). \emph{Local} caps also bound the
count within each group $g$: $\sum_{i\in g}\mathbf{1}[\hat y_i=c]\le K_g$ (the same
percentage of the group's own class count). Because the indicator is not
differentiable, training uses the \emph{soft count} $S=\sum_i p_\theta(y=c\mid x_i)$,
while satisfaction is verified with the \emph{hard count} from $\arg\max$.

\paragraph{A bounded transductive penalty.}
The defining choice in TraLO is that the constraint penalty is \emph{bounded}. For a
soft excess $E = \max(0,\, S - K)$ over a cap $K$, the per-constraint penalty is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\ell(E;K,\rho)\;=\;\underbrace{\frac{E}{E+K}}_{\text{rational saturation}}
\;+\;\rho\,\underbrace{\frac{(E/K)^2}{1+(E/K)^2}}_{\text{bounded quadratic}} ,
\end{equation}
where both terms saturate as $E\!\to\!\infty$ (the first to $1$, the second to $\rho$).
What actually drives training is the penalty's \emph{gradient} on the count; differentiating
Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} through $E=\max(0,S-K)$ gives, for $S>K$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:grad}
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial S}
= \frac{K}{(E+K)^2} + \frac{2\rho}{K}\,\frac{E/K}{\bigl(1+(E/K)^2\bigr)^2}.
\end{equation}
Both the value and this gradient are uniformly bounded, and that bound is what makes the
constraint pressure controllable.

\begin{proposition}[Bounded, non-escalating pressure]
\label{prop:bound}
For $E\ge 0$ and $\rho\ge 0$, the penalty of Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}
obeys $0\le\ell<1+\rho$, and its count-gradient lies in
$[0,\,(1+\rho)/K]$: strongest ($\to 1/K$) at the boundary $E{=}0$, and vanishing as
$E\to\infty$. Hence with the multiplier frozen at $\lambda$, each cap adds at most
$\lambda(1+\rho)$ to the loss and at most $\lambda(1+\rho)/K$ to the gradient norm,
both \emph{independent of the violation size}.
\end{proposition}

\noindent (Proof: elementary calculus, Supp.~Sec.~A.) A linear penalty $\lambda E$ instead
contributes a constant gradient and a quadratic one $\rho(E/K)^2$ a gradient $2\rho E/K^2$
unbounded in $E$, so both force the multiplier to escalate to keep pace with cross-entropy
(Fig.~\ref{fig:loss_shape}). Proposition~\ref{prop:bound} bounds the constraint pressure and
keeps it from escalating with the violation; that a \emph{small} frozen multiplier then
suffices to hold the count ($\lambda_g\!\le\!0.09$ across our grid) is an empirical finding,
not part of the proposition, as are the constrained-class quality gains, which the ablation
attributes to the optimizer reset and hinge (Supp.~Sec.~B) rather than the penalty shape.
The total objective combines cross-entropy with the global and local penalties (and,
after satisfaction, the undershoot hinge introduced below),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total}
L_{\text{total}} = L_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_g\,\ell_g + \lambda_l\,\ell_l ,
\end{equation}
where $\ell_g=\ell(E;K,\rho)$ is the global-cap penalty and $\ell_l$ sums
Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} over the group caps; every cap (the global one and each group
cap) carries its own multiplier, so $\lambda_l\,\ell_l$ abbreviates
$\sum_g \lambda_{l,g}\,\ell(E_g;K_g,\rho)$.

\paragraph{Multiplier ratchet and freeze.}
Rather than dual ascent, $\lambda$ is \emph{ratcheted}: it increases by a fixed step
each epoch \emph{only while the hard count violates the cap}, and is \emph{frozen} the
moment the count is first satisfied. The bounded penalty means a small frozen multiplier
suffices (empirically $\lambda_g\!\le\!0.09$), so cross-entropy is never swamped.
Multipliers start at $0.05$. The
quadratic weight $\rho$ ramps linearly (from $5$ toward $100$) until first
satisfaction and then also freezes. Because $\lambda$ and $\rho$ both stop moving at
first satisfaction, TraLO is really a two-phase procedure: while the count is violated it
\emph{searches} for a sufficient multiplier by ratcheting $\lambda$ (the bounded gradient
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad} keeps that search ending at a small $\lambda$), and once the count
is met it \emph{settles}. Formally, for every epoch $t\ge T_{\text{sat}}$ (first
satisfaction) the coefficients $\lambda_g,\lambda_l,\rho$ are constant, so Phase~2 is plain
SGD on one fixed objective $F(\theta)=L_{\text{CE}}+\lambda_g\ell_g+\lambda_l\ell_l$. Under
the standard assumptions for smooth nonconvex SGD it therefore converges to a first-order
stationary point of $F$ \citep{ghadimi2013stochastic,bottou2018optimization}; $\ell$ meets
the gradient bound of Prop.~\ref{prop:bound} and is smooth except on the measure-zero
boundary $\{S_c{=}K_c\}$, which a softplus in place of the ReLU would remove. A dual or
augmented-Lagrangian update instead changes the objective every step, so its iterate chases
a moving target and inherits no such guarantee without extra assumptions on the multiplier
sequence. What Proposition~\ref{prop:bound} does \emph{not} give is feasibility from the
loss: since $\ell$
saturates, no finite $\lambda$ makes it an exact penalty \citep{bertsekas2014constrained}, so
TraLO does not enforce the hard count through $\ell$ alone. It need not: the ratchet raises
$\lambda$ only until the $\arg\max$ count is met, and the post-hoc step
(Alg.~\ref{alg:tralo}) closes any residual soft-to-hard gap. The guarantee is thus one of
\emph{stability}, a bounded and non-escalating penalty on a fixed Phase-2 objective, not
penalty-driven feasibility.

\paragraph{Optimizer reset at satisfaction.}
At the first epoch the count is met, we reset the Adam optimizer state. This removes the
accumulated descent momentum that would otherwise keep pushing the soft count far below
the cap (over-satisfying at the cost of constrained-class recall). The reset lets the
frozen-multiplier objective settle near the boundary instead of overshooting inward. We
motivate the reset empirically; the ablation (Supp.~Sec.~B) isolates its effect, and we do
not claim to derive it.

\paragraph{Undershoot hinge.}
A satisfied cap can still leave the model under-predicting the constrained class,
which costs recall. Concretely, each cap $c$ adds $\lambda_c\,\beta\,\max(0,\,K_c-S_c)/K_c$ to the loss
with $\beta{=}0.5$. It is zero whenever the soft count sits at or above its cap, so it
can never re-violate. The effect: after satisfaction this one-sided hinge gently pushes the
soft count back up toward (but not over) the cap whenever it has undershot,
restoring constrained-class recall without re-violating the count. Supp.~Sec.~B
shows the hinge is one of the two components that carry the effect, together with the
optimizer reset.

\paragraph{Training schedule.}
Training has a warmup phase (cross-entropy only, to a stable initialization that is
cached and reused) followed by the constraint phase under Eq.~\eqref{eq:total}. A
post-hoc verification step is available to enforce a hard count if a run has not landed
natively; for TraLO it is essentially never needed (Supp.~Sec.~C).
Algorithm~\ref{alg:tralo} summarizes the loop.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{TraLO training loop}
\label{alg:tralo}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\STATE warm up $\theta$ on $L_{\text{CE}}$ alone; cache the warmup model
\FOR{each constraint epoch}
  \STATE update $\theta$ on $L_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_g\,\ell_g + \lambda_l\,\ell_l$ (plus the hinge once satisfied) \hfill (Eq.~\ref{eq:total})
  \STATE recount hard predictions on the test inputs
  \IF{a cap is violated}
    \STATE ratchet that cap's $\lambda$ up one step; advance $\rho$
  \ELSIF{first time all caps are met}
    \STATE freeze $\lambda$ and $\rho$; reset the Adam state
  \ENDIF
  \STATE stop after $5$ consecutive satisfied epochs
\ENDFOR
\STATE post-hoc verification (flip borderline predictions) if needed
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.}
We use three MedMNIST v2 \citep{yang2023medmnist} classification tasks with their
official splits, each with a
naturally rare class that makes a count cap meaningful (Figure~\ref{fig:datasets} shows
DermMNIST examples): \textbf{TissueMNIST} (8 classes,
constrained class GE, $\approx\!7\%$ of the test set), \textbf{DermMNIST} (7 skin-lesion
classes from HAM10000 \citep{tschandl2018ham10000}, constrained class melanoma, with
per-group local caps), and \textbf{OctMNIST} (4 retinal-OCT classes, constrained class
drusen). Caps are set as a percentage $L$ of the
constrained class's true test-set count (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}); for the local-cap (asymmetric)
study we also vary the group cap $G$ independently of $L$. The percentage
parameterization uses the class's true count only to standardize how hard the cap
binds across datasets; the method itself receives an exogenous integer $K$, exactly
as a deployed capacity would be given.

\paragraph{Backbones.}
The headline grid uses three backbones, MobileNetV3 \citep{howard2019searching},
RegNetY-400MF \citep{radosavovic2020designing}, and a ViT-B/16 transformer
\citep{dosovitskiy2021image}, to show the findings are not CNN-specific.
All backbones use standard ImageNet-style training conventions \citep{he2016deep}.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Two \emph{post-hoc clippers}: a greedy threshold \textbf{Heuristic} and a
linear-programming clipper \textbf{Shifman-LP} \citep{shifman2025classification}. Two
constraint-\emph{trained} dual-ascent methods: \textbf{Fioretto-LDF}
\citep{fioretto2020lagrangian} and \textbf{Hounie-RCL} \citep{hounie2023resilient}. We
also report \textbf{TraLO-bounded}, our own ablation with the undershoot hinge switched
off, used both as a method and as the within-cell control in Supp.~Sec.~B.

\paragraph{Baseline adaptations.}
The two trained baselines were built for a different setting, so we re-instantiated each
on the transductive count task and ran it inside TraLO's exact harness: the same cached
warmup, the same $300$-epoch budget and stop rule (five consecutive feasible epochs), the
same gradient clipping, and the same post-hoc verification step at the end. Two details
keep the comparison fair. We point each method's constraint gradient at the unlabeled test
set, since that is where the count is enforced, whereas the original papers target a
training or population expectation. And we select each method's reported checkpoint on the
constraint-excess axis (best feasible, else lowest excess), never on F1, so no baseline can
quietly keep its best-F1 epoch. \textbf{Fioretto-LDF} is otherwise unchanged: a linear
penalty $\lambda_c S_c$ with per-constraint subgradient ascent
$\lambda_c\!\leftarrow\!\lambda_c+\eta\max(0,S_c\!-\!K_c)$ on the test counts.
\textbf{Hounie-RCL} keeps its primal/slack/dual updates, with the constraint residual read
as $(S_c\!-\!K_c)/N$. The one substantive fix in our port is to normalize its primal
count-gradient by the sample count so the primal step and the dual update share a
scale. Otherwise the primal term is $N\times$ too strong and over-suppresses the capped
class. Its step sizes are fixed a priori, not tuned per cell, and swept only to confirm
fairness (Supp.~Sec.~A). \textbf{Shifman-LP} is post-hoc only: a global-and-local linear program that allocates the
capped labels on the warmup model's test-set softmax, using an identity misclassification
cost rather than the general cost matrix of the original two-phase allocator \citep{shifman2025classification}.

\paragraph{Frozen recipe.}
All methods share one protocol, fixed before the final runs: ImageNet-pretrained
backbones, $224{\times}224$ inputs, batch size $64$, no augmentation, Adam with
learning rate $10^{-4}$ during warmup and $5{\times}10^{-6}$ in the constraint
phase; warmup $=50$ epochs (cross-entropy only), constraint phase $=300$ epochs,
seeds $1$--$4$ (all reported, no seed selection). Per-method constraint steps were
fixed a priori, not tuned per cell: dual step $0.005$ (Fioretto-LDF) and dual and
relaxation steps $0.01$ (Hounie-RCL) on TissueMNIST and DermMNIST, and $0.01$ and $0.1$
respectively on OctMNIST; ratchet step $0.002$ with hinge weight
$\beta{=}0.5$ (TraLO). A step-fairness sweep (Supp.~Sec.~A) confirms the OctMNIST
result is insensitive to these choices. The headline grid is
\emph{paper\_final}: $3$ backbones $\times\,3$ datasets $\times\,9$ cap levels
$\times\,6$ methods $\times\,4$ seeds $=1944$ runs. Models are implemented in PyTorch
with torchvision backbones and MedMNIST v2 loaders, and each run trains on a single
NVIDIA GPU. Code, the configuration generators,
and the full per-run results corpus accompany the submission as the Code and Data
Supplement.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Our \emph{primary} metrics are quality: \textbf{constrained-class F1 (cc-F1)}, the quality
of the capped class itself and the quantity the constraint most directly puts at risk, and
\textbf{macro-F1} for overall quality. As \emph{secondary} deployment diagnostics we also
report \textbf{native satisfaction} (the fraction of runs whose final model meets the global
count with no post-hoc edit) and \textbf{flips} (the number of test predictions the post-hoc
verification step overwrites to enforce the global and per-group caps; $0$ when the run needs
no adjustment); we treat these as deployment \emph{properties} rather than headline metrics,
since every deployable method meets the hard count one way or another
(Supp.~Sec.~C). Gaps versus baselines are \emph{paired} by seed: because the seeds are
matched, we summarize a method's advantage by its seed-winrate and a paired Wilcoxon test
rather than by marginal $\pm$std bars, which would understate a paired effect when a
baseline is seed-unstable but its per-seed gap to TraLO is consistent.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Constrained-class quality vs.\ the trained baselines}
\label{sec:oct}

We start with constrained-class F1 (cc-F1), the quality of predictions on the very
class the cap restricts. Against the constraint-trained baselines the
summary is a tie almost everywhere, and a win where the cap binds hard.

\paragraph{The hard regime: OctMNIST tight caps.}
The one place TraLO pulls clearly ahead of the trained baselines is OctMNIST at
tight caps, where the cap binds hard but the drusen class is still discriminable. There,
TraLO leads the best trained baseline on cc-F1 at every backbone, and the margin
\emph{grows with backbone capacity}: $+0.016$/$+0.022$ on MobileNetV3 ($L30$/$L40$),
$+0.035$/$+0.021$ on RegNet, and $+0.081$/$+0.051$ on the ViT-B/16 transformer, at a
$4/4$ seed winrate on every backbone; pooled over the six tight-cap cells TraLO wins
all $24$ paired seeds (paired Wilcoxon $p<10^{-6}$), and the same cells improve macro-F1 at
five of the six (mean $+0.012$, up to $+0.028$ on the ViT; Table~\ref{tab:oct_backbone}).
Figure~\ref{fig:octmnist} plots the full sweep:
on a representative backbone (top) the six methods nearly coincide, but the per-backbone
gap (bottom) reveals the tight-binding win and its honest inverted-U: near-zero at
$L10$/$L20$ and at loose caps, positive only where the cap binds. The post-hoc clippers'
higher raw cc-F1 in the band is bought by editing predictions (Supp.~Sec.~C).

\paragraph{Robustness of the OctMNIST win.}
Three checks. (i) \emph{Backbone-general}: in leave-one-backbone-out cross-validation
(define the winning band on one backbone, test on the held-out one), every held-out
backbone confirms the win at $100\%$ winrate (pooled held-out gap $+0.049$).
(ii) \emph{Re-derivable without the gap}: the winning band is recovered by a rule that
never looks at the TraLO-vs-baseline gap, ``binding (a clipper must flip $\ge 65$
predictions) and discriminable (constrained-class F1 $\in[0.35,0.55]$)'', under
which HIT cells win $100\%$ and MISS cells $0$--$40\%$. (iii) \emph{Honest shape}: the
effect is an inverted-U in tightness, not ``tighter is always better,'' and it is
specific to OctMNIST; the same rule does not transfer to tissue/derm. We claim this as
the one \emph{backbone-general} cc-F1 win; the regime map (Sec.~\ref{sec:regime}) adds
one preliminary win at global-tighter asymmetric caps (on two of the three backbones), and
a broad tie elsewhere, not a blanket cc-F1 advantage.

\subsection{Wins, ties, and losses across the full grid}
\label{sec:regime}

Beyond the symmetric grid, we score the full corpus (the \emph{paper\_final} grid on
the three backbones plus dedicated MobileNetV3 asymmetric sweeps), partitioned into five
constraint-geometry regimes, with every regime reported so nothing is cherry-picked; the
full per-cell breakdown is in Supp.~Sec.~D. A regime counts as a \emph{win} only when the
mean paired cc-F1 gap and at least half its cells clear $+0.005$, and a \emph{loss} under
the mirror-image rule; no regime mean falls below $-0.005$. The pattern is consistent:
\textbf{TraLO wins where the count is hard to hit and ties where it is slack.} Only two
regimes clear the win bar: OctMNIST tight-symmetric caps (the backbone-general win of
Sec.~\ref{sec:oct}, largest on ViT-B/16) and the global-tighter asymmetric ($G{<}L$)
caps of DermMNIST ($+0.8$pp on MobileNetV3, reproduced on RegNet and tied on ViT;
\emph{preliminary}, as only MobileNetV3 has the full asymmetric grid). Every other
regime, including all symmetric caps, is a statistical tie (symmetric tissue+derm mean
cc-F1 gap $-0.0013$, seed winrate $29\%$, $n{=}216$), and none is a loss. Against the
post-hoc clippers macro-F1 is higher for TraLO on every regime (Sec.~\ref{sec:deploy}).

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Quality vs.\ post-hoc clipping}
\label{sec:deploy}

The other baseline family is the field default: train an unconstrained model and
clip the highest-scoring predictions down to the cap. Both TraLO and clipping end
up meeting the count; the question is what quality that costs. Because clipping
meets the cap only by overwriting predictions (often dozens per run), it pays an
accuracy tax that TraLO, landing on the count natively, avoids. Across the full
$1944$-run grid (run-level, paired by seed, against the best post-hoc clipper)
this is a macro-F1 gain for TraLO of $+0.053$ (Tissue), $+0.016$ (Derm) and $+0.021$
(OctMNIST), positive in every regime and in $77$ of the $81$ symmetric cells
(Supp.~Sec.~D; the exceptions are the very tightest $L10$/$L20$ caps), with
constrained-class F1 moving the same way in aggregate. The margin over clipping is a
property of training under the constraint rather than of TraLO alone. The dual
baselines post nearly identical macro-F1 margins (the lone exception a tie at
OctMNIST$\times$ViT), so we claim it for the
constraint-trained family; what is TraLO's own is reaching that quality with the
fastest and most reliable native convergence (Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}) and the fewest
prediction edits at binding caps (Supp.~Sec.~C). The tax is steepest where the cap
binds: at the tight $L30$ cap, clipping costs up to $0.061$ macro-F1
(Tissue; Supp.~Table~S2). We report how TraLO avoids the tax (near-perfect
native satisfaction and an order of magnitude fewer prediction edits) as a deployment
property in Supp.~Sec.~C: what separates the methods is the quality each
preserves while meeting the count.

% ----------------------------------------------------------------------------
\subsection{Convergence}
\label{sec:conv}

These advantages all rest on one behavior, that TraLO reaches the count during
training. Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} tracks how far each
method's prediction count sits above the cap over the constraint phase, on the cell where
the three methods separate most cleanly: DermMNIST on RegNet, whose cross-entropy
saturates slowly enough that the count and the task objective openly compete. TraLO's
ratcheted multiplier pushes from the first constraint epoch and reaches the cap near epoch
$12$, while the zero-start dual baselines lag (Fioretto-LDF near epoch $21$, Hounie-RCL near
epoch $38$). The gap is not specific to this cell. Over the full grid, native feasibility is $100\%$
for TraLO and Hounie-RCL but only $87\%$ for Fioretto-LDF, whose unbounded multiplier stalls
short of the cap on the rest. Across DermMNIST and TissueMNIST on the two convolutional
backbones and the $17$ symmetric-cap cells we log where the cap binds, median epochs-to-feasibility is $17$/$20$/$44$ for
TraLO/Fioretto-LDF/Hounie-RCL, with Hounie-RCL slowest in every cell and TraLO first or tied in $13$
of the $17$; the per-cell census accompanies the released corpus. TraLO alone meets the count both fast and on every run, which is what lets it
avoid the post-hoc editing step that costs clipping its quality.

\section{Conclusion}

Count caps are a real, common deployment constraint, and the two standard answers each
cost something: post-hoc clipping meets the cap but pays an accuracy tax for editing
predictions, while dual-ascent training chases the cap with an unbounded multiplier and can
stall short of it. TraLO addresses this with a bounded transductive penalty, a multiplier
frozen at satisfaction, an optimizer reset, and an undershoot hinge. It matches the strong
constraint-trained baselines across the grid and beats them on the constrained class where
the cap binds hard: robustly on OctMNIST tight caps, where the lead grows with backbone
capacity and reaches $+0.081$ on the ViT, and preliminarily at the global-tighter asymmetric
caps of DermMNIST. The ablation traces this to the optimizer reset and hinge ($n{=}24$
paired, $p\!\le\!3\mathrm{e}{-6}$; Supp.~Sec.~B). Against post-hoc clipping it improves
macro-F1 on all three datasets by landing on the count natively.

\paragraph{Limitations.}
The main limitation is scope: the cc-F1 advantage over trained baselines is regime- and
dataset-specific (a tie elsewhere), and the global-tighter asymmetric win on DermMNIST
is preliminary (it reproduces on RegNet but ties on the ViT, and only MobileNetV3 has the
full asymmetric grid), so the one backbone-general win is OctMNIST tight caps. All three
datasets are MedMNIST-derived ($28{\times}28$ upsampled to $224$); the count constraint
itself is resolution-agnostic (it acts on prediction counts, not pixels). TraLO is also
transductive: it uses the known test inputs during training, which fits deployments where
the batch to be scored is fixed in advance (a triage queue, an audit pool) but not
streaming or shifting-test settings, where the caps would be re-solved as the pool changes.
Our analysis is also self-contained: it characterizes TraLO's own optimization but does not
\emph{theoretically} rank it against augmented-Lagrangian, proximal, PID, or mirror-descent
solvers, which would need a dedicated empirical comparison.

\paragraph{Broader applicability.}
Nothing in the formulation is specific to medical imaging: TraLO enforces a global cap on the
number of inputs a classifier assigns to a class, plus per-group sub-caps, the structure of any
capacity-limited decision system (a dispatch queue with a fixed budget; slots allocated across
applicant groups). These are structural analogies, not validated applications; what carries over
is meeting the quota during training so the model respects it natively. Validating TraLO outside
medical imaging, at full resolution and wider geometries, is the natural next step.
