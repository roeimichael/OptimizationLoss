\begin{abstract}
A deployed classifier often faces a hard cap on how many items of a known evaluation
batch it may assign to a constrained class (a screening pool from which only so many
cases can be escalated), optionally with per-group local caps. We call this a
\emph{transductive count constraint}. Post-hoc clipping meets the cap but erodes accuracy
by editing predictions, while dual-ascent training escalates its multiplier and can stall
short of feasibility. We propose \textbf{TraLO}, which trains through the count with a
bounded penalty, a multiplier that ratchets while the count is violated and freezes once
it is met, an optimizer reset at first feasibility, and an undershoot hinge that restores
constrained-class recall. Across three MedMNIST datasets and three backbones, TraLO
matches the strong constraint-trained baselines over most of the grid and pulls ahead
of them on constrained-class F1 where the count binds hard and the class stays
discriminable: on OctMNIST tight caps the lead is backbone-general and largest on the
ViT-B/16. Grafting the reset and hinge onto the dual baselines transfers most of that
lead, while the penalty's saturating shape proves neutral, so the gain lives in the two
portable components, not the formulation the method is named for. Against post-hoc
clipping, the constraint-trained family improves macro-F1 on nearly every dataset and
backbone while landing on the cap natively. We report the full regime map, wins and ties
alike.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

A surprising number of classification systems are deployed under a constraint not
on which items get a label but on how many do. A screening program reads a fixed pool
of scans and may escalate only so many of them to specialist review, and an audit team
can act on only so many flagged filings in a quarter. In each case the operational requirement is a
\emph{hard upper bound on the count} of predictions in a constrained class over the
evaluation set. The items to be labeled are known in advance (a fixed batch to be
scored, not a stream), so the
constraint is naturally \emph{transductive}: it is a property of the whole test-set
prediction vector, not of any single example.

The standard way to honor such a cap is \emph{post-hoc clipping}: train a normal
model, rank the test items by their score for the constrained class, keep the top-$K$,
and reassign the rest. This is trivial and always meets the count, so it is the realistic
deployment baseline. But it meets the count by
editing predictions, often dozens of them, each a forced error
on an item the model was confident about, so accuracy degrades. The other
end of the spectrum is \emph{constrained training}: encode the cap as a constraint and
solve the Lagrangian with dual ascent
\citep{fioretto2020lagrangian,hounie2023resilient,chamon2020probably}. In principle this
is exactly the right formulation, but for a hard count the dual multiplier of a
linear penalty climbs without bound: a one-epoch-warmup probe shows it climbing steeply
while our bounded multiplier barely moves, for the same final count (Supp.~Sec.~A). The
implementations remain competitive, and we do not over-read the escalation. It is a
symptom, not the damage: it buys no faster descent and does not predict where TraLO wins.
What it marks is a formulation that on some cells never reaches feasibility at all
(Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}).

We propose \textbf{TraLO} (Transductive Loss Optimization), a constrained-training
method whose multiplier stays bounded by construction. Its transductive penalty is
bounded (a rational-saturation term plus a bounded quadratic in the soft excess
over the cap), the multiplier only moves upward while the count is violated
and freezes the first time it is met, the Adam state is reset at first satisfaction,
and an undershoot hinge restores constrained-class recall once the cap is met. The
result is a classifier that lands on the count by itself, with no prediction surgery.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}\itemsep2pt
  \item \textbf{A deployable formulation and method.} We cast the operational
  ``$\le K$ predictions of class $c$'' requirement as a transductive count constraint
  and give a bounded penalty with a freeze-at-satisfaction multiplier,
  optimizer reset, and undershoot hinge (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}).
  \item \textbf{A characterized quality win over the trained baselines.}
  Versus the much stronger constraint-trained baselines, TraLO matches
  constrained-class F1 across most of the grid and beats them where the count
  binds hard and the constrained class stays discriminable: on all three backbones
  at the OctMNIST tight caps, with the largest lead on the ViT-B/16. We publish the
  full regime map, including the large
  tie region (Secs.~\ref{sec:oct}--\ref{sec:regime}).
  \item \textbf{A quantified accuracy tax for post-hoc clipping, and a family that
  avoids it.} The constraint-trained family, TraLO included, improves macro-F1 over the
  field-default clipping baseline on nearly every dataset and backbone
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:deploy}), with native satisfaction on every run in our grid
  (Supp.~Sec.~C).
  \item \textbf{Mechanism, ablation, graft.} The bounded penalty reaches the count with
  a small frozen multiplier; the quality gain is carried by the optimizer reset and
  hinge, isolated by ablation (Supp.~Sec.~B) and portable by graft: added to the dual
  baselines they transfer most of the tight-cap gain, an anti-windup dual restart none
  (Sec.~\ref{sec:graft}).
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}

\paragraph{Lagrangian and dual constrained learning.}
Encoding requirements as constraints solved with primal--dual methods is the
classical recipe \citep{bertsekas1982constrained}, brought to deep networks by
Lagrangian-duality training \citep{fioretto2020lagrangian} and given statistical
footing by PAC constrained-learning theory
\citep{chamon2020probably,chamon2022constrained}. Resilient Constrained Learning
\citep{hounie2023resilient} adapts the constraint level to make hard requirements
attainable; PID-style dual updates stabilize the multiplier in safe RL
\citep{stooke2020responsive}; and constraints can beat fixed penalties on sparsity
targets \citep{gallego2022controlled}. Our critique of runaway escalation is thus
specific to the unbounded linear penalty: augmented-Lagrangian and resilient updates are
better behaved, yet the resilient method we benchmark still winds up slowly on a hard
count (Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}). Our two dual-ascent baselines are Fioretto-LDF
\citep{fioretto2020lagrangian} and Hounie-RCL \citep{hounie2023resilient}; TraLO instead
optimizes a bounded penalty and freezes the multiplier at satisfaction.

\paragraph{Output-level and fairness constraints.}
Closer to our output-level setting, prediction constraints have been enforced
during training by primal--dual updates \citep{nandwani2019primal} and by linear
label-count constraints for weakly supervised segmentation
\citep{pathak2015constrained}, or guaranteed per prediction by
input-dependent parametrizations \citep{brosowsky2021sample} or an output-layer
satisfiability solver \citep{goyal2024deepsade}. \citet{marquez2017imposing} find hard
enforcement feasible but no better than soft penalties, motivating our bounded
penalty. Constrained optimization has protected under-represented classes
\citep{sangalli2021constrained}, and
group-wise constraints underpin fair classification
\citep{bilal2015fairness,agarwal2018reductions}, which our local caps resemble
structurally, though a local cap is a \emph{ceiling}, not a fairness floor
(Sec.~\ref{sec:setup}).

\paragraph{Rate-constrained learning.}
The closest classical relatives constrain prediction \emph{rates}:
coverage as a dataset constraint
\citep{goh2016satisfying}, non-decomposable rate objectives through surrogate bounds
\citep{eban2017scalable}, non-differentiable rate constraints through proxy-Lagrangian
games \citep{cotter2019optimization}, and generalized rate metrics through multi-player
schemes \citep{narasimhan2019optimizing}. These bound \emph{expected} rates over a
distribution, inductively; ours is a hard count on one specific test set, verified on
the argmax predictions and paired with per-group local caps.

\paragraph{Post-hoc correction and resource allocation.}
Resource-constrained classification allocates a fixed budget over a trained
classifier's outputs at decision time, classically as a small assignment
problem, most recently under exactly our local-plus-global structure
\citep{shifman2025classification}. Related lines fold the budget into
learning via adaptive cost-sensitive weights \citep{shifman2023adaptive} or
predict-and-optimize ranking under stochastic capacity
\citep{vanderschueren2024new}, and learning-with-rejection \citep{cortes2016learning}
prices abstention per instance, all steering expected costs rather than
a hard count. Decision-time
allocation always meets the budget by editing predictions; our
post-hoc baselines are a greedy threshold heuristic and a linear-programming clipper
(Shifman-LP, after \citealt{shifman2025classification}).

\paragraph{Cost-sensitive and imbalanced training.}
Reweighting or reshaping the loss changes which examples are
emphasized: cost-sensitive thresholds \citep{elkan2001foundations}, focal loss
\citep{lin2017focal}, class-balanced weights \citep{cui2019class},
label-distribution-aware margins \citep{cao2019learning}, prior-corrected logits
\citep{menon2020long}. None guarantees the prediction count, which TraLO targets directly.

\paragraph{Transductive and set-level constraints.}
Transductive inference reasons about a fixed unlabeled test set
\citep{vapnik1998statistical,joachims1999transductive}, where soft set-level targets
have long existed: expectation regularization pushes predicted class
proportions toward a prior \citep{mann2007simple}, and
posterior regularization constrains posterior expectations
\citep{ganchev2009posterior}. Our constraint is transductive
in this sense but \emph{hard}: an explicit argmax count budget, with per-group
caps, enforced during training.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\paragraph{Problem.}
Let the (known) test set contain $N$ items and let $p_\theta(y=c\mid x_i)$ be the
model's probability for the constrained class $c$ on item $i$. A \emph{global} count
constraint requires the number of items predicted $c$ to satisfy
$\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathbf{1}[\hat y_i = c] \le K$. We parameterize the cap as a percentage
$L$ of the constrained class's true test-set count, so $L$ measures how much of the
class's actual demand the quota admits ($L30$ admits at most $30\%$ of the true count).
\emph{Local} caps also bound the
count within each group $g$: $\sum_{i\in g}\mathbf{1}[\hat y_i=c]\le K_g$ (the same
percentage of the group's own class count). Because the indicator is not
differentiable, training uses the \emph{soft count} $S=\sum_i p_\theta(y=c\mid x_i)$,
while satisfaction is verified with the \emph{hard count} from $\arg\max$.

\paragraph{A bounded transductive penalty.}
The defining choice in TraLO is that the constraint penalty is \emph{bounded}. For a
soft excess $E = \max(0,\, S - K)$ over a cap $K$, the per-constraint penalty is
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:loss}
\ell(E;K,\rho)\;=\;\underbrace{\frac{E}{E+K}}_{\text{rational saturation}}
\;+\;\rho\,\underbrace{\frac{(E/K)^2}{1+(E/K)^2}}_{\text{bounded quadratic}} ,
\end{equation}
where both terms saturate as $E\!\to\!\infty$ (the first to $1$, the second to $\rho$).
What actually drives training is the penalty's \emph{gradient} on the count; differentiating
Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} through $E=\max(0,S-K)$ gives, for $S>K$,
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:grad}
\frac{\partial \ell}{\partial S}
= \frac{K}{(E+K)^2} + \frac{2\rho}{K}\,\frac{E/K}{\bigl(1+(E/K)^2\bigr)^2}.
\end{equation}
Both the value and this gradient are uniformly bounded, which keeps the
constraint pressure controllable. The total objective combines cross-entropy with the
global and local penalties (and, after satisfaction, the undershoot hinge introduced below),
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total}
L_{\text{total}} = L_{\text{CE}} + \lambda_g\,\ell_g + \lambda_l\,\ell_l ,
\end{equation}
where $\ell_g=\ell(E;K,\rho)$ is the global-cap penalty and $\ell_l$ sums
Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} over the group caps; every cap (the global one and each group
cap) carries its own multiplier, so $\lambda_l\,\ell_l$ abbreviates
$\sum_g \lambda_{l,g}\,\ell(E_g;K_g,\rho)$.

The three statements that follow are elementary: we state them to chart the design space of
Eq.~\eqref{eq:total}, synthesizing standard facts rather than claiming new theory.

\begin{lemma}[Bounded, saturating pressure]
\label{lem:bound}
For $E\ge 0$ and $\rho\ge 0$, the penalty of Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} obeys $0\le\ell<1+\rho$,
and its count-gradient obeys $0\le\partial\ell/\partial S\le(1+\rho)/K$, vanishing as
$E\to\infty$. Hence with the multiplier frozen at $\lambda$, each cap adds at most
$\lambda(1+\rho)$ to the loss, and its pull on the soft count is at most $\lambda(1+\rho)/K$:
a bounded, saturating pressure \emph{independent of the violation size}.
\end{lemma}

\noindent The proof is a short calculus argument (Supp.~Sec.~A; there the count-gradient
peaks in the interior, not at $E{=}0$, whenever $\rho\gtrsim0.8$, as throughout our grid). A
linear penalty $\lambda E$ instead contributes a gradient constant in $E$, and dual ascent
drives $\lambda$ upward with no ceiling. The bound also shapes the \emph{updates}, not just
the objective:

\begin{proposition}[Rank-one constraint gradient]
\label{prop:rank1}
Each cap's penalty depends on the parameters $\theta$ only through its scalar soft-count
$S_c(\theta)=\sum_{i\in I_c}p_\theta(y{=}c\mid x_i)$. By the chain rule,
$\nabla_\theta[\lambda_c\,\ell(E_c;K_c,\rho)]=a_c\,\nabla_\theta S_c$ with
$a_c=\lambda_c\,\partial\ell/\partial S_c\ge 0$: at any fixed $\theta$, a nonnegative scalar
times the direction $\nabla_\theta S_c$, and that direction is fixed with respect to
$\lambda_c$, $\rho$, and the shape of $\ell$. The full constraint term therefore lies in the
span of $\{\nabla_\theta S_c\}_c$, a subspace of dimension at most the number of caps.
\end{proposition}

\noindent So the multiplier, $\rho$, and the shape of $\ell$ set only the \emph{magnitude}
$a_c$ of a cap's push at a given $\theta$, never its direction. This has a sharp consequence
once the step is norm-clipped:

\begin{corollary}[Clipping erases the multiplier]
\label{cor:clip}
Clip the total gradient to max-norm $\tau$. In the regime where one cap's term dominates,
$\lVert a_c\nabla_\theta S_c\rVert>\tau$ and $\lVert\nabla_\theta L_{\text{CE}}\rVert$ is
negligible beside it, the clipped step equals $\tau\,\nabla_\theta
S_c/\lVert\nabla_\theta S_c\rVert$, independent of $\lambda_c$, $\rho$, and the shape of
$\ell$ \citep{pascanu2013difficulty}.
\end{corollary}

\noindent In that regime an escalating multiplier cannot change the update: a matched probe
with $\lambda$ differing ${\sim}280\times$ leaves training nearly indistinguishable
(Supp.~Sec.~A). The weight $\rho$ and the shape reshape $a_c$ rather than rescale it, so we
test them too, and both prove neutral (Sec.~\ref{sec:graft}; Supp.~Sec.~B). Adam's
accumulated state and the undershoot hinge are \emph{not} rescalings; they drive a
\emph{different} direction, and the quality rests on them.
\looseness=-1

\paragraph{Multiplier ratchet and freeze.}
Rather than dual ascent, $\lambda$ is \emph{ratcheted}: it increases by a fixed step
each epoch \emph{only while the hard count violates the cap}, and is \emph{frozen} the
moment the count is first satisfied. The bounded penalty means the search ends at a small
frozen multiplier rather than an escalating one; multipliers start at $0.05$. The
quadratic weight $\rho$ ramps linearly (from $5$ toward $100$) until first
satisfaction and then also freezes. Because $\lambda$ and $\rho$ both stop moving at
first satisfaction, TraLO is really a two-phase procedure: while the count is violated it
\emph{searches} for a sufficient multiplier by ratcheting $\lambda$ (the bounded gradient
of Eq.~\eqref{eq:grad} keeps that search ending at a small $\lambda$), and once the count
is met it \emph{settles}.

\paragraph{Stability, and why a verification step is retained.}
Freezing $\lambda$ and $\rho$ at first satisfaction turns the moving-target dual problem into
a \emph{single fixed} objective $F(\theta)=L_{\text{CE}}+\lambda_g\ell_g+\lambda_l\ell_l$:
this is the stability the design buys, the tail of training descending one objective rather
than chasing a multiplier that is still moving. That objective is cross-entropy plus a fixed
additive penalty of bounded gradient (Lemma~\ref{lem:bound}), smooth away from the caps, so
the frozen phase is no worse conditioned than the cross-entropy training the backbone already
undergoes. What the penalty is \emph{not} is smooth \emph{at} the cap, and that is what makes
feasibility subtle:

\begin{proposition}[Kinked penalty, exact at a finite threshold]
\label{prop:exact}
The composed penalty $S\mapsto\ell(\max(0,S{-}K);K,\rho)$ is nonsmooth at the cap: its
left-derivative there is $0$ and its right-derivative is $1/K$, independent of $\rho$. Hence
along a path on which the soft count rises through $K$, with cross-entropy preferring a larger
count (pull $c=\lvert dL_{\text{CE}}/dS\rvert$ at the cap), the frozen objective
$F=L_{\text{CE}}+\lambda\ell$ has a local minimum \emph{at} $S{=}K$ once $\lambda\ge cK$: the
penalty is of exact-penalty type, feasible at a finite multiplier.
\end{proposition}

\noindent This is the classical exactness of a nonsmooth penalty with nonzero boundary slope
\citep{bertsekas1982constrained,nocedal2006numerical}, and it explains why TraLO reaches the
count natively: once cross-entropy saturates (the shared accuracy gate) the pull $c$
collapses, so any frozen $\lambda>0$ clears the threshold. The design does not lean on this.
The ratchet freezes $\lambda$ at the first \emph{hard}-count satisfaction, which need not
reach $cK$, and the penalty acts on the \emph{soft} count while feasibility is defined by the
\emph{hard} count; the verification step (Supp.~Sec.~C) closes that residual soft-versus-hard
gap.

\paragraph{Optimizer reset at satisfaction.}
At the first epoch the count is met, we reset the Adam optimizer state. This removes the
accumulated descent momentum that would otherwise keep pushing the soft count far below
the cap (over-satisfying at the cost of constrained-class recall). The reset lets the
frozen-multiplier objective settle near the boundary instead of overshooting inward. It
is motivated empirically, not derived; the ablation (Supp.~Sec.~B) isolates its effect.

\paragraph{Undershoot hinge.}
A satisfied cap can still leave the model under-predicting the constrained class,
which costs recall. Concretely, each cap $c$ adds $\lambda_c\,\beta\,\max(0,\,K_c-S_c)/K_c$ to the loss
with $\beta{=}0.5$. It is zero whenever the soft count sits at or above its cap, so it
can never re-violate; after satisfaction it gently pushes an undershooting soft count
back up toward (but not over) the cap, restoring constrained-class recall.
Supp.~Sec.~B shows the hinge and the reset together carry the effect.

\paragraph{Training schedule.}
Training has a warmup phase (cross-entropy only, to a stable initialization that is
cached and reused) followed by the constraint phase under Eq.~\eqref{eq:total}. A
post-hoc verification step is available to enforce a hard count if a run has not landed
natively; for TraLO it is essentially never needed (Supp.~Sec.~C).
Supplementary Algorithm~S1 summarizes the loop.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Datasets.}
We use three MedMNIST v2 \citep{yang2023medmnist} classification tasks with their
official splits (Figure~\ref{fig:datasets} shows DermMNIST examples):
\textbf{TissueMNIST} (8 classes, constrained class GE, $7.1\%$ of the test set),
\textbf{DermMNIST} (7 skin-lesion classes from HAM10000
\citep{tschandl2018ham10000}, constrained class melanoma, $11.1\%$), and
\textbf{OctMNIST} (4 retinal-OCT classes, constrained class drusen, $25\%$ of a
class-balanced test set). The first two carry the minority class the screening motivation
assumes; OctMNIST does not, and we report it because the cap binds hardest there. Local
caps use real groups only on DermMNIST (HAM10000 anatomical site:
torso/extremity/head-neck); TissueMNIST and OctMNIST lack group metadata and use a fixed
synthetic split, a mechanism test rather than a fairness setting (Supp.~Sec.~E). Caps
are set as a percentage $L$ of the
constrained class's true test-set count (Sec.~\ref{sec:method}); the asymmetric
study also varies the group cap $G$ independently of $L$. The percentage form only
standardizes how hard the cap binds across datasets; the method itself receives an
exogenous integer $K$, exactly as a deployed capacity would be given.

\paragraph{Backbones.}
The headline grid uses three backbones, MobileNetV3 \citep{howard2019searching},
RegNetY-400MF \citep{radosavovic2020designing}, and a ViT-B/16 transformer
\citep{dosovitskiy2020image}, to show the findings are not CNN-specific.
All backbones use standard ImageNet-style training conventions \citep{he2016deep}.

\paragraph{Baselines.}
Two \emph{post-hoc clippers}: a greedy threshold \textbf{Heuristic} and a
linear-programming clipper \textbf{Shifman-LP} \citep{shifman2025classification}. Two
constraint-\emph{trained} dual-ascent methods: \textbf{Fioretto-LDF}
\citep{fioretto2020lagrangian} and \textbf{Hounie-RCL} \citep{hounie2023resilient}. We
also report \textbf{TraLO-bounded}, our own ablation with the undershoot hinge switched
off, used both as a method and as the within-cell control in Supp.~Sec.~B.

\paragraph{Baseline adaptations.}
The two trained baselines were built for a different setting, so we re-instantiated each
on the transductive count task and ran it inside TraLO's exact harness: the same cached
warmup, $300$-epoch budget and stop rule (five consecutive feasible epochs), gradient
clipping, and post-hoc verification step. Two details are specific to the port: each
method's constraint gradient targets the unlabeled test set, where the count is enforced
(the originals target a training or population expectation), and each method's reported
checkpoint is selected on the constraint-excess axis (best feasible, else lowest
excess), never on F1, so no baseline is credited with its best-F1 epoch. \textbf{Fioretto-LDF} is otherwise unchanged: a linear
penalty $\lambda_c S_c$ with per-constraint subgradient ascent
$\lambda_c\!\leftarrow\!\lambda_c+\eta\max(0,S_c\!-\!K_c)$ on the test counts.
\textbf{Hounie-RCL} keeps its primal/slack/dual updates, with the constraint residual read
as $(S_c\!-\!K_c)/N$. The one substantive fix in our port is to normalize its primal
count-gradient by the sample count so the primal step and the dual update share a
scale. Otherwise the primal term is $N\times$ too strong and over-suppresses the capped
class. \textbf{Shifman-LP} is post-hoc only: a global-and-local linear program that allocates the
capped labels on the warmup model's test-set softmax, using an identity misclassification
cost rather than the general cost matrix of the original two-phase allocator \citep{shifman2025classification}.

\paragraph{Frozen recipe.}
All methods share one protocol, fixed before the final runs: ImageNet-pretrained
backbones, $224{\times}224$ inputs, batch size $64$, no augmentation, Adam with
learning rate $10^{-4}$ during warmup and $5{\times}10^{-6}$ in the constraint
phase; warmup $=50$ epochs (cross-entropy only), constraint phase $=300$ epochs,
seeds $1$--$4$ (all reported, no seed selection). Compute is modest: the constraint
phase runs a median $2$--$7$ minutes per run on one GPU for TraLO and the dual baselines
alike, on top of a shared cached warmup (clipping itself costs seconds but pays the
quality tax of Sec.~\ref{sec:deploy}). Per-method constraint steps were
fixed a priori, not tuned per cell: dual step $0.005$ (Fioretto-LDF), dual and
relaxation steps $0.01$ (Hounie-RCL) on all three datasets, and ratchet step $0.002$ with
hinge weight $\beta{=}0.5$ (TraLO). A step-fairness sweep (Supp.~Sec.~A) varies the
baseline steps over a $10\times$ range, and a matching sweep varies TraLO's own $\beta$ and
$\lambda_{\text{step}}$ (Supp.~Sec.~B): the OctMNIST tight-cap win is
insensitive to both (the best baseline variant still trails by $0.019$; TraLO clears the
baseline at every arm). The headline grid is
\emph{paper\_final}: $3$ backbones $\times\,3$ datasets $\times\,9$ cap levels
$\times\,6$ methods $\times\,4$ seeds $=1944$ runs. Models are implemented in PyTorch
with torchvision backbones and MedMNIST v2 loaders, and each run trains on a single
commodity NVIDIA GPU, with no multi-GPU or distributed training. Code, the configuration generators,
and the full per-run results corpus accompany the submission as the Code and Data
Supplement.

\paragraph{Metrics.}
Our \emph{primary} metrics are quality: \textbf{constrained-class F1 (cc-F1)}, the quality
of the capped class itself and the quantity the constraint most directly puts at risk, and
\textbf{macro-F1} for overall quality. As \emph{secondary} deployment diagnostics we also
report \textbf{native satisfaction} (the fraction of runs whose final model meets the global
count with no post-hoc edit) and \textbf{flips} (the number of test predictions the post-hoc
verification step overwrites to enforce the global and per-group caps; $0$ when the run needs
no adjustment); we treat these as deployment \emph{properties} rather than headline metrics,
since every deployable method meets the hard count one way or another
(Supp.~Sec.~C). Gaps versus baselines are \emph{paired} by seed: because the seeds are
matched, we summarize a method's advantage by its seed-winrate and a paired Wilcoxon test
rather than by marginal $\pm$std bars, which would understate a paired effect when a
baseline is seed-unstable but its per-seed gap to TraLO is consistent. Claims spanning
several cells are tested on cell means (cells are the independent units; the four seeds
recur across cells); within one cell, $n{=}4$ comparisons are reported as effect sizes
and seed counts, since a four-pair Wilcoxon cannot fall below $p{=}0.125$.

\section{Results}

\subsection{Constrained-class quality vs.\ the trained baselines}
\label{sec:oct}

We start with constrained-class F1 (cc-F1), the quality of predictions on the very
class the cap restricts; Table~\ref{tab:ccf1} shows the full symmetric grid, all three
datasets over all three backbones, on both headline metrics (cc-F1 here, macro-F1 in
Sec.~\ref{sec:deploy}). TraLO's sharpest result is where the count binds
hard: at OctMNIST tight caps it leads the best constraint-trained baseline on every
backbone, by a mean of $+0.038$ across the six cells (sign test $p{=}0.031$) and peaking
at $+0.081$ on the ViT-B/16 (per-backbone paired deltas in Supp.~Table~S6). Where the cap is slack the
summary is a tie: TraLO is best-or-tied against the two trained baselines in $23$ of the
$27$ cells ($22$ when our own TraLO-bounded ablation variant is also counted as a
competitor), and on tissue and derm the differences at equal feasibility are within seed noise
(paired Wilcoxon, not significant), a tie that also holds away from the grid, at a
tighter ($L40$) and a looser ($L60$) cap on RegNet.

\paragraph{The hard regime: OctMNIST tight caps.}
The one place TraLO pulls clearly ahead of the trained baselines is OctMNIST at
tight caps, where the cap binds hard but the drusen class is still discriminable. There,
TraLO leads the best trained baseline on cc-F1 at every backbone and every seed
($4/4$ per cell, $24$ of $24$ paired comparisons overall), with the margin largest on
the ViT-B/16; the six tight-cap
deltas are in Supp.~Table~S6. Treating cells as the independent unit, the six cell-mean
gaps are all positive; the $t$-test on the cell means gives $p{=}0.013$, and the same
cells improve macro-F1 at five of the six (mean $+0.012$).
The win is recall at the cap, not precision: TraLO matches the best trained baseline on
constrained-class precision ($0.90$ vs $0.91$) and beats it on recall ($0.31$ vs $0.27$),
the quantity the undershoot hinge restores. A tight cap holds recall near its ceiling
$K/N$ at high precision, and training recovers the leftover headroom: a property of the cap,
not the $28{\times}28$ resolution. Figure~\ref{fig:octmnist} plots the sweep: the six
methods nearly coincide (top), while the per-backbone gap (bottom) reveals the win and its
inverted-U. The post-hoc clippers' higher raw cc-F1 in the band is bought by editing
predictions (Supp.~Sec.~C).

\paragraph{Robustness of the OctMNIST win.}
Three checks. (i) \emph{Backbone-general}: leave-one-backbone-out cross-validation
(define the winning band on the two training backbones, test on the held-out one; script
in the supplement) holds on every held-out backbone: pooled held-out gap $+0.033$ at a
$93\%$ seed-winrate, and $100\%$ on the tight caps themselves.
(ii) \emph{Re-derivable without the gap}: a rule blind to the TraLO-vs-baseline
gap, ``binding ($\ge 65$ forced flips) with recall headroom at high precision'', selects
exactly the winning cells (HIT $100\%$, MISS $0$--$40\%$; label-derived, analysis not
deployment). (iii) \emph{Inverted-U, not monotone}: the effect is an
inverted-U in tightness, not ``tighter is always better,'' and it is OctMNIST-specific.
This is the one \emph{backbone-general} cc-F1 win; the full regime map follows
(Sec.~\ref{sec:regime}).

\paragraph{Which components carry the win? A graft experiment.}
\label{sec:graft}
Two controls rerun the six tight-cap cells under the frozen recipe, warmup caches, and
one GPU model ($144$ runs; re-run baselines reproduce the main grid to $\pm0.0013$;
per-cell table in Supp.~Sec.~B). \emph{Graft}: giving each dual baseline exactly TraLO's
optimizer reset and undershoot hinge (its own penalty and multipliers kept) lifts
Fioretto-LDF's cc-F1 in six of six cells ($+0.019$ to $+0.073$) and Hounie-RCL's in
five of six. Measured seed-by-seed against the stronger of the two grafted baselines,
TraLO's edge is only $+0.0015$ and it trails in two of the six cells: once the recipe is
grafted the constraint-trained methods are essentially interchangeable, which is exactly
our claim: the reset and hinge, not TraLO's formulation, carry the gain. \emph{Anti-windup}: Fioretto-LDF with dual restarts
\citep{gallego2022controlled} deploys predictions identical to plain Fioretto-LDF in
every cell (the deployed checkpoint is fixed before first feasibility), and TraLO beats
it in all six, so the deficit is not missing anti-windup machinery. \emph{Penalty shape}:
swapping Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss}'s saturating penalty for a linear one at the same ratcheted
multiplier moves cc-F1 by $-0.001$ (the bounded shape wins $9$ of $24$ runs and loses $9$,
with $6$ exact ties; sign-test $p{=}1.0$) and feasibility by two epochs, consistent with
the fixed-direction argument of Sec.~\ref{sec:method}. The quality mechanism is the reset
plus hinge, portable across hosts; Eq.~\eqref{eq:loss} buys a small, readable multiplier,
not the gains.

\subsection{Wins, ties, and losses across the full grid}
\label{sec:regime}

Across the full corpus the pattern is consistent:
\textbf{TraLO wins where the count binds hard \emph{and} the constrained class stays
discriminable, and ties where either condition fails.} Binding alone is not enough: at
$L30$ the warmup model over-demands the cap by $2.4$--$4.8\times$ on tissue and derm,
more than OctMNIST's $2.0$--$2.5\times$, yet only OctMNIST clears the win bar. We score
the \emph{paper\_final} grid on the three backbones plus dedicated MobileNetV3 asymmetric
sweeps, partitioned into five constraint-geometry regimes, every regime reported
(per-cell breakdown in Supp.~Sec.~D). A regime counts as a
\emph{win} only when the mean paired cc-F1 gap and at least half its cells clear $+0.005$,
and a \emph{loss} under the mirror-image rule; no regime mean falls below $-0.005$. Only two
regimes clear the win bar: OctMNIST tight-symmetric caps (the backbone-general win of
Sec.~\ref{sec:oct}, largest on ViT-B/16) and the global-tighter asymmetric ($G{<}L$)
caps of DermMNIST ($+0.8$pp on MobileNetV3, reproduced on RegNet and tied on ViT;
\emph{preliminary}, as only MobileNetV3 has the full asymmetric grid). Every other
regime, including all symmetric caps, is a statistical tie (symmetric tissue+derm mean
cc-F1 gap $-0.0013$, seed winrate $29\%$, $n{=}216$), and none is a loss. Against the
post-hoc clippers macro-F1 is higher for TraLO on every regime (Sec.~\ref{sec:deploy}).

\subsection{Quality vs.\ post-hoc clipping}
\label{sec:deploy}

The other baseline family is the field default: train unconstrained, clip the
top-scoring predictions down to the cap. Both end up meeting the count; the question
is what quality that costs. Clipping meets the cap by overwriting predictions (often
dozens per run), an accuracy tax that TraLO, landing on the count natively, avoids.
Across the full
$1944$-run grid (run-level, paired by seed, against the best post-hoc clipper)
this is a macro-F1 gain for TraLO of $+0.053$ (Tissue), $+0.016$ (Derm) and $+0.021$
(OctMNIST), positive in every regime and in $77$ of the $81$ symmetric cells
(per cell at the displayed caps in the macro-F1 block of Table~\ref{tab:ccf1};
Supp.~Sec.~D has all $81$; the exceptions are the very tightest $L10$/$L20$ caps), with
constrained-class F1 moving the same way in aggregate. The margin over clipping is a
property of training under the constraint rather than of TraLO alone: the dual
baselines post nearly identical macro-F1 margins (the lone exception a tie at
OctMNIST$\times$ViT), so we claim it for the constraint-trained family; constraint-time
training also adapts to the test batch, a confound we do not separate from avoided
editing. What is TraLO's
own is reaching that quality with the fastest and most reliable native convergence
(Sec.~\ref{sec:conv}) and the fewest prediction edits at binding caps. The tax is
steepest where the cap binds: at the tight $L30$ cap, clipping costs up to $0.061$
macro-F1 (Tissue; Supp.~Table~S3). How TraLO avoids the tax (near-perfect native
satisfaction, an order of magnitude fewer edits) is a deployment property reported in
Supp.~Sec.~C: what separates the methods is the quality each preserves while meeting
the count.

\subsection{Convergence}
\label{sec:conv}

These advantages rest on TraLO reaching the count during
training. Figure~\ref{fig:convergence} tracks how far each
method's prediction count sits above the cap over the constraint phase, on the cell where
the three methods separate most cleanly: DermMNIST on RegNet, whose cross-entropy
saturates slowly enough that the count and the task objective openly compete. TraLO's
ratcheted multiplier acts from the first constraint epoch and reaches the cap well before
either zero-start dual baseline (per-method epochs in the caption). The gap holds
grid-wide: native feasibility is $100\%$
for TraLO and Hounie-RCL but only $87\%$ for Fioretto-LDF, whose unbounded multiplier stalls
short of the cap on the rest. Across DermMNIST and TissueMNIST on the two convolutional
backbones, over the $18$ symmetric-cap cells we log, Hounie-RCL is the slowest of the three
in every one and TraLO is never the slowest: TraLO reaches the count first or within one
epoch in $15$ of the $18$ (strictly first in $11$), at median epochs-to-feasibility of $17$,
$20$, and $49.5$ for TraLO, Fioretto-LDF, and Hounie-RCL. Fioretto-LDF is competitive when
it converges but stalls on the runs it does not, so TraLO is the only method that reaches
the count both fast and on every run, which lets it avoid the editing that costs clipping
its quality. The per-cell census accompanies the released corpus.

\section{Conclusion}

Count caps are a real, common deployment constraint whose two standard answers each
cost something: post-hoc clipping pays an accuracy tax for editing
predictions, and dual-ascent training chases the cap with an escalating multiplier and,
in its linear-penalty form, can stall short of it. TraLO trains through the count with a
bounded penalty, a multiplier frozen at satisfaction, an optimizer reset, and an
undershoot hinge. It matches the strong constraint-trained baselines across the grid and
beats them on the constrained class where the cap binds hard and the class stays
discriminable (OctMNIST tight caps; preliminarily, DermMNIST's
global-tighter asymmetric caps), and improves macro-F1 over post-hoc clipping while
landing on the count natively. An ablation and a cross-method graft trace that gain to
the reset and hinge, which transfer to the dual baselines; the penalty's own shape proves
neutral (Sec.~\ref{sec:graft}).

\paragraph{Limitations and broader impact.}
The advantage over trained baselines is regime-specific, and the DermMNIST asymmetric win
is MobileNetV3-only. All three datasets are MedMNIST-derived ($28{\times}28$ upsampled to
$224$); full-resolution validation and a theoretical ranking against other solvers remain
future work. TraLO is transductive, scoped to a batch scored in one shot, not a stream. A
cap trades recall for capacity, and our local caps are \emph{ceilings}, not
fairness \emph{floors} \citep{hardt2016equality}.
